Accelerate · Introduction

Techno-optimism

Includes effective accelerationism, the network state, progress studies, the abundance agenda and augmentation

Techno-optimism holds that technology and markets drive human progress, and that AI, if not held back, will make people richer, healthier and more capable.

Francis Bacon tied knowledge to power over nature, and Friedrich Hayek praised markets for using knowledge that no planner could hold. In 2023 Marc Andreessen’s manifesto joined technology and markets in what Nick Land had called the “techno-capital machine”. It named its enemies, from stagnation and central planning to the precautionary principle.

Augmentation is its moderate strand: AI as a tool that extends people rather than replacing them. It runs from Vannevar Bush’s memex through Douglas Engelbart, a patron saint of the manifesto, to Steve Jobs’s “bicycle for the mind”. Today it speaks through AI copilots and Reid Hoffman’s “bloomers”, optimists who want to steer AI as they build it.

Effective accelerationism, or e/acc, is its radical strand. It began in 2022 among pseudonymous posters on X, among them “Beff Jezos”, later named as the physicist Guillaume Verdon, and grounds growth in physics rather than in human welfare. Its network-state wing, led by Balaji Srinivasan, would exit existing states to found new ones.

Who holds it now

Marc Andreessen

Co-founder of the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z). Wrote “Why AI Will Save the World” and “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto” in 2023.

Leading the Future

A network of super PACs launched in August 2025 as a $100 million effort, backed by Andreessen Horowitz, OpenAI's Greg Brockman and others. It targets the 2026 midterms and opposes policies it says stifle innovation.

Reid Hoffman

Co-founder of LinkedIn and investor. His 2025 book Superagency, with Greg Beato, argues that AI gives people new powers. He calls himself a bloomer.

Satya Nadella

Chief executive of Microsoft, which sells its AI assistants as Copilot. In December 2025 he wrote that AI should be a scaffold for human potential, not a substitute for it.

Patrick Collison and Tyler Cowen

Entrepreneur and economist. In 2019 they called for Progress Studies, a field to learn how progress happens and how to speed it up.

Peter Thielsince 2009

Investor and PayPal co-founder. He argues that technology has stagnated since the 1970s. In lectures from 2025 he cast those who want to regulate AI as servants of the Antichrist.

Jason Crawford

Founder of the Roots of Progress Institute. His Techno-Humanist Manifesto, serialised from 2024, values technology for what it does for human flourishing.

Promises

A market society led by founders, with a light state, and at its edge an exit into opt-in network states. It echoes classical liberalism and its libertarian edge.

If Techno-optimism’s demands became law tomorrow, the rules would come off. Open models, data centres and automation would spread fast, and the gains would arrive quickly, along with disruption to work.

Interests

Much of its money comes from a few philanthropists. Open Philanthropy has given the Institute for Progress $9.3 million in general support since 2021, and Patrick and John Collison helped launch the Roots of Progress Institute.

Good Ventures, the foundation behind Open Philanthropy, put $60 million into a $120 million Abundance and Growth Fund, with Patrick Collison among the other backers.

Its demands could serve those who build and own the technology. The largest platforms and labs gain, since the belief makes any rule look like a cost in lives. Start-ups and open-source developers gain too, because fewer rules lower the cost of entry.

Founder-investors could gain most directly, as growth flows first to those who own the capital, and parties of deregulation gain a moral cause. Energy firms gain from the data-centre build-out.

Regulators would lose power, since every rule must justify its cost in lives, and safety, ethics and labour bodies would lose standing. Established firms exposed to automation would lose their place.

2026Milestone · the network state

The Network School moves to Kazakhstan

Balaji Srinivasan

Srinivasan opened the Network School in 2024 at Forest City in Johor, Malaysia, across the strait from Singapore. It is a co-living community for founders and creators, part of his effort to build a network state. In July 2026 the local council revoked its business licence, citing breaches of licensing and premises rules. The next day Srinivasan signed a five-year memorandum with Kazakhstan's Ministry of Artificial Intelligence and Digital Development, and announced a new campus there.

Angelica Ang, “Booted from Malaysia, tech guru Balaji Srinivasan finds a new patron for his ‘nomad’ commune: Kazakhstan”, Fortune, 26 July 2026; Nan Hidayat Nan Azmie, “Network School heading to Kazakhstan after Johor operations halted”, The Star, 22 July 2026

2025Milestone · augmentation

Superagency

Reid Hoffman and Greg Beato

The co-founder of LinkedIn argues, with Greg Beato, that AI will give people new superpowers. When many people gain them at once, everyone benefits, a state the book calls superagency. Hoffman sorts views of AI into doomers, gloomers, zoomers and bloomers. Bloomers, like him, are cautious optimists who want AI in many hands and shaped by its users' feedback.

Reid Hoffman and Greg Beato, Superagency: What Could Possibly Go Right with Our AI Future, Authors Equity, 28 January 2025; Angie Basiouny, “Embracing AI: Are You a Doomer, Gloomer, Zoomer or Bloomer?”, Knowledge at Wharton, 4 February 2025; Anthony Ha, TechCrunch, 26 January 2025

2025Milestone · e/acc

Thermodynamic chips

Extropic, led by Guillaume Verdon

Verdon's start-up, founded in 2022 with Trevor McCourt, unveils its first chips and a development platform already tested by early partners. The chips are built from probabilistic bits, circuits whose output wanders at random between 1 and 0 with programmable odds. In simulations on small benchmarks, Extropic claims, its approach could be up to 10,000 times more energy-efficient than GPUs. Once it succeeds, it says, “energy constraints will no longer limit AI scaling”.

Extropic, “Thermodynamic computing from zero to one” and “Inside X0 and XTR-0”, extropic.ai, 2025; Extropic, “Thermodynamic Hardware”, extropic.ai; Maximilian Schreiner, “Startup Extropic plans to revolutionize AI hardware by embracing the power of randomness”, The Decoder, 2 April 2024

2023Modern definition

“The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”

Marc Andreessen

In fifteen sections, mostly statements of belief, the co-founder of Andreessen Horowitz argues that progress comes from technology combined with free markets. Together they form what Nick Land called the techno-capital machine. On AI, it states that “any deceleration of AI will cost lives”. Its enemies include stagnation, central planning and the precautionary principle, and it counts tech ethics among the names of a demoralisation campaign.

Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, Andreessen Horowitz (a16z.com), 16 October 2023

“Why AI Will Save the World”

Marc Andreessen

Andreessen argues that AI will not destroy the world and may save it, by augmenting human intelligence. “Every child will have an AI tutor that is infinitely patient,” he writes. 2022Modern definition · e/acc

“Notes on e/acc principles and tenets”

Beff Jezos and Bayeslord

In point-form notes on Substack, the two set out the thinking behind e/acc. Life arose through dissipative adaptation, they argue, and intelligence is a faster form of the same process, capitalism a form of intelligence. Against top-down control they favour competition, and welcome Balaji Srinivasan's network states as tests of how to govern. Some in e/acc, Beff among them, call themselves post-humanists, and their summary says to stop fighting “the thermodynamic will of the universe”.

Beff Jezos and Bayeslord, “Notes on e/acc principles and tenets”, Beff's Newsletter, Substack, 10 July 2022

2022Milestone · the network state

The Network State

Balaji Srinivasan

The former chief technology officer of Coinbase publishes his book on 4 July, also free to read online. A network state, he writes, is “a highly aligned online community with a capacity for collective action”. It crowdfunds territory around the world and in time wins diplomatic recognition from existing states. Six days later, e/acc's tenets said the idea particularly excited them.

Balaji Srinivasan, The Network State, 2022, thenetworkstate.com; Balaji Srinivasan, “The Network State Book”, balajis.com, 19 March 2022, updated 4 July 2022; Beff Jezos and Bayeslord, “Notes on e/acc principles and tenets”, 10 July 2022

2022Coined the word

Effective accelerationism is named

“swarthy” (@zestular), with @BasedBeffJezos, @bayeslord and @creatine_cycle

At the end of May, a handful of pseudonymous Twitter users announce a new philosophy, its name a play on effective altruism. A newsletter post by swarthy defines it and sets it apart from EA, which it finds unfocused and too averse to risk. Its first line repeats a sentence from Nick Land's 2017 introduction to accelerationism. Humanity's overarching goal, it says, is “to preserve the light of consciousness”.

swarthy, newsletter post on Substack (swarthy.substack.com), 31 May 2022, reposted verbatim as “Effective Accelerationism — e/acc”, e/acc newsletter, 31 October 2022; “e/acc (Effective Accelerationism)”, Know Your Meme; Hasan Chowdhury, “Get the lowdown on ‘e/acc’ …”, Business Insider, 28 July 2023; Nick Land, “A Quick-and-Dirty Introduction to Accelerationism”, Jacobite, 25 May 2017

2020Milestone

“It's Time to Build”

Marc Andreessen

Early in the coronavirus pandemic, Andreessen blames the West's lack of tests, ventilators and masks on “our widespread inability to build”. He calls for housing, highly automated factories, nuclear reactors and high-speed trains, and for new ways to deliver education and health care. The problem, he argues, is a lack of desire and will, along with inertia and regulatory capture.

Marc Andreessen, “It's Time to Build”, Andreessen Horowitz (a16z.com), 18 April 2020

2019Milestone

A new science of progress

Patrick Collison and Tyler Cowen

An entrepreneur and an economist call for a field that studies how progress happens and how to speed it up. By progress they mean economic, technological, scientific, cultural and organisational advance. Such advances, they note, arose in tiny pockets, from Renaissance Florence to Silicon Valley. “We suggest inaugurating the discipline of ‘Progress Studies’,” they write.

Patrick Collison and Tyler Cowen, “We Need a New Science of Progress”, The Atlantic, 30 July 2019; excerpted by Tyler Cowen on Marginal Revolution, 31 July 2019

2013Milestone · the network state

“Silicon Valley's Ultimate Exit”

Balaji Srinivasan

At Y Combinator's Startup School, a founder of the genetic-testing company Counsyl contrasts two responses to a declining system. Voice means changing it from within, and exit means leaving to build something new. “Exit amplifies voice,” he argues, and technology should help people opt out peacefully. Reason noted that he was echoing Albert O. Hirschman's 1970 book Exit, Voice, and Loyalty.

Balaji Srinivasan, “Silicon Valley's Ultimate Exit”, Startup School, Y Combinator, October 2013; J. D. Tuccille, “Tech Should Make It Easier To Escape Government Control, Says Startup Veteran Balaji Srinivasan”, Reason, 30 October 2013; Y Combinator, “Videos from Startup School 2013 are now online”, 27 October 2013

2013Claimed ancestor · cited · e/acc

Dissipation-driven adaptation

Jeremy England

An MIT physicist derives the least heat that any self-replicating system must give off. He goes further, arguing that matter driven by an outside energy source will often reorganise itself to dissipate more energy. Harvard's Eugene Shakhnovich found it interesting but “extremely speculative” as an account of life. e/acc's 2022 tenets cite England for their claim that life emerged through dissipative adaptation.

Jeremy L. England, “Statistical Physics of Self-Replication”, The Journal of Chemical Physics 139 (12), 121923, 2013; Natalie Wolchover, “A New Physics Theory of Life”, Quanta Magazine, 22 January 2014; Beff Jezos and Bayeslord, “Notes on e/acc principles and tenets”, 2022

2012Milestone

Abundance

Peter Diamandis and Steven Kotler

A co-founder of Singularity University and his co-author argue that exponential technologies will soon end scarcity. AI, robotics, digital manufacturing and synthetic biology will let humanity “meet and exceed the basic needs of every person on the planet”. The book takes water, food, energy, health care, education and freedom in turn.

Peter H. Diamandis and Steven Kotler, Abundance: The Future Is Better Than You Think, Free Press, 21 February 2012; publisher's description, Simon & Schuster

2012Milestone · neoreaction

The Dark Enlightenment

Nick Land

In a long essay posted online in parts, Land surveys the blogger Mencius Moldbug and his readers, whom he calls neo-reactionaries. Its first part is headed “Neo-reactionaries head for the exit”. Against democratic voice it sets exit, the freedom to leave, and it adopts Moldbug's name for the ruling order, the Cathedral. It also quotes Peter Thiel's doubt that freedom and democracy are compatible.

Nick Land, “The Dark Enlightenment”, thedarkenlightenment.com, 2012, archived copy

2010Claimed ancestor · cited

What Technology Wants

Kevin Kelly

The founding executive editor of Wired calls the whole system of human technology the technium. It grows almost like a living thing, he argues, with tendencies of its own. Each invention adds options and choices, and that growth of possibility is his definition of progress. Andreessen names Kelly among the patron saints of techno-optimism.

Kevin Kelly, What Technology Wants, Viking, 18 October 2010; Kevin Kelly, “The Positive Balance of Technology”, The Technium, 1 March 2011; Kirkus Reviews, 2010; Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, 2023 (patron saints)

“The Education of a Libertarian”

Peter Thiel

In an essay for Cato Unbound, the PayPal co-founder writes, “I no longer believe that freedom and democracy are compatible.” The great task for libertarians, he argues, is to find a way out of politics altogether. Unqualified Reservations

Curtis Yarvin, as Mencius Moldbug

James Dale Davidson and William Rees-Mogg

Its authors predict that the internet and digital money will let the richest and most able slip free of the nation state. A bicycle for the mind

Steve Jobs

In a presentation filmed around 1980, Apple's co-founder compares the personal computer to a bicycle. In a study of how efficiently species move, he recalls, the condor beat humans, but a human on a bicycle beat the condor. Anti-Oedipus

Gilles Deleuze and Félix Guattari

A philosopher and a psychoanalyst publish the first volume of Capitalism and Schizophrenia. Types of civilisation

Nikolai Kardashev

Writing on how alien civilisations might send signals, a Soviet astrophysicist ranks them by the power they command. “Augmenting Human Intellect”

In a report for the US Air Force Office of Scientific Research, Engelbart of the Stanford Research Institute sets out a programme. 1940s

1945Claimed ancestor · cited

“The Use of Knowledge in Society”

Friedrich Hayek

The Austrian-born economist argues that no central planner can gather the knowledge an economy needs. It exists only as “dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge” held by many people. Prices pass it on, like a system of telecommunications. The Techno-Optimist Manifesto invokes Hayek's knowledge problem against any centralised economy.

F. A. Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society”, American Economic Review 35(4), September 1945, pp. 519–530; Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, 2023 (Hayek's Knowledge Problem; patron saints)

1945Attributed by historians · augmentation

“As We May Think”

Vannevar Bush

The magazine introduces Bush as coordinator of some six thousand leading American scientists in the war. He asks what scientists should do next, and answers that the growing store of knowledge must be easier to reach. He imagines the memex, a desk holding a person's books and records, “an enlarged intimate supplement to his memory”. Engelbart's 1962 report devotes a section to the idea.

Vannevar Bush, “As We May Think”, The Atlantic Monthly, July 1945; Douglas C. Engelbart, Augmenting Human Intellect, 1962, sections III.A.1–2; Howard Rheingold, Tools for Thought, Simon & Schuster, 1985, chapter 9

The Manifesto of Futurism

Filippo Tommaso Marinetti

On the front page of Le Figaro, an Italian poet glorifies speed, struggle and war, “the world's only hygiene”. A racing car, he declares, is more beautiful than the Victory of Samothrace. The 2023 Techno-Optimist Manifesto names him a patron saint and adapts his seventh point, putting technology where he wrote poetry. Marinetti went on to co-write the Fascist Manifesto of 1919.

England's Lord Chancellor proposes a new method for the sciences, to give people power over nature. “Human knowledge and human power meet in one,” runs his third aphorism, and nature must be obeyed to be commanded.
