Techno-optimism

Includes augmentation

Techno-optimism holds that technology and markets drive human progress, and that AI, if not held back, will make people richer, healthier and more capable.

Francis Bacon tied knowledge to power over nature, and Friedrich Hayek praised markets for using knowledge that no planner could hold. In 2023 Marc Andreessen’s manifesto joined technology and markets in what Nick Land had called the “techno-capital machine”. It named its enemies, from stagnation and central planning to the precautionary principle.

Augmentation is its moderate strand: AI as a tool that extends people rather than replacing them. It runs from Vannevar Bush’s memex through Douglas Engelbart, a patron saint of the manifesto, to Steve Jobs’s “bicycle for the mind”. Today it speaks through AI copilots and Reid Hoffman’s “bloomers”, optimists who want to steer AI as they build it.

Who holds it now

Marc Andreessen

Co-founder of the venture firm Andreessen Horowitz (a16z). Wrote “Why AI Will Save the World” and “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto” in 2023.

Leading the Future

A network of super PACs launched in August 2025 as a $100 million effort, backed by Andreessen Horowitz, OpenAI's Greg Brockman and others. It targets the 2026 midterms and opposes policies it says stifle innovation.

Reid Hoffman

Co-founder of LinkedIn and investor. His 2025 book Superagency, with Greg Beato, argues that AI gives people new powers. He calls himself a bloomer.

Satya Nadella

Chief executive of Microsoft, which sells its AI assistants as Copilot. In December 2025 he wrote that AI should be a scaffold for human potential, not a substitute for it.

Patrick Collison and Tyler Cowen

Entrepreneur and economist. In 2019 they called for Progress Studies, a field to learn how progress happens and how to speed it up.

Jason Crawford

Founder of the Roots of Progress Institute. His Techno-Humanist Manifesto, serialised from 2024, values technology for what it does for human flourishing.

Who disputes it

Janus Rose noted in Vice that one of the manifesto's patron saints, Marinetti, co-wrote the Fascist Manifesto of 1919 (2023). Daron Acemoglu and Simon Johnson reply that power and choices decide who gains from technology (Power and Progress, 2023). They want AI that helps workers, and say much of the industry aims to replace them. Vitalik Buterin, who offers his own nuanced techno-optimism, holds that AI is “fundamentally different from other tech” and calls for defensive acceleration (2023).

2020s

2025Milestone · augmentation

“Bicycles for the mind”, again

Satya Nadella

Microsoft's chief executive starts a personal blog with a post on the year ahead. He calls for a new concept that evolves Steve Jobs's bicycles for the mind. People will be equipped with “cognitive amplifier” tools, he writes, and AI should support human potential, not substitute for it. The Register noted that getting customers to pay for Copilot and Microsoft's other AI services remained “a work in progress”.

Satya Nadella, “Looking Ahead to 2026”, sn scratchpad, 29 December 2025; quoted by Julie Bort, TechCrunch, 5 January 2026, and dated by Luis Rijo, PPC Land, 2 January 2026; Thomas Claburn, The Register, 2 January 2026

2025Milestone · augmentation

Superagency

Reid Hoffman and Greg Beato

The co-founder of LinkedIn argues, with Greg Beato, that AI will give people new superpowers. When many people gain them at once, everyone benefits, a state the book calls superagency. Hoffman sorts views of AI into doomers, gloomers, zoomers and bloomers. Bloomers, like him, are cautious optimists who want AI in many hands and shaped by its users' feedback.

Reid Hoffman and Greg Beato, Superagency: What Could Possibly Go Right with Our AI Future, Authors Equity, 28 January 2025; Angie Basiouny, “Embracing AI: Are You a Doomer, Gloomer, Zoomer or Bloomer?”, Knowledge at Wharton, 4 February 2025; Anthony Ha, TechCrunch, 26 January 2025

2023Modern definition

“The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”

Marc Andreessen

In fifteen sections, mostly statements of belief, the co-founder of Andreessen Horowitz argues that progress comes from technology combined with free markets. Together they form what Nick Land called the techno-capital machine. On AI, it states that “any deceleration of AI will cost lives”. Its enemies include stagnation, central planning and the precautionary principle, and it counts tech ethics among the names of a demoralisation campaign.

Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, Andreessen Horowitz (a16z.com), 16 October 2023

2023Milestone

“Why AI Will Save the World”

Marc Andreessen

Andreessen argues that AI will not destroy the world and may save it, by augmenting human intelligence. “Every child will have an AI tutor that is infinitely patient,” he writes. AI has no goals of its own, he says, and he calls the fear that it will kill us a cult. The greatest risk, he argues, is that China wins, so the West should build fast and keep open source free.

Marc Andreessen, “Why AI Will Save the World”, Andreessen Horowitz (a16z.com), 6 June 2023

2023Milestone · augmentation

“Your copilot for work”

Microsoft

Microsoft builds an AI assistant called Copilot into Word, Excel, PowerPoint, Outlook and Teams. It is meant to work alongside people, who decide what to keep. “With Copilot, you're always in control,” the launch post promises. GitHub had previewed its Copilot in 2021, an AI “pair programmer” built on OpenAI's Codex.

Jared Spataro, “Introducing Microsoft 365 Copilot – your copilot for work”, Official Microsoft Blog, 16 March 2023; Nat Friedman, “Introducing GitHub Copilot: your AI pair programmer”, GitHub Blog, 29 June 2021

2020Milestone

“It's Time to Build”

Marc Andreessen

Early in the coronavirus pandemic, Andreessen blames the West's lack of tests, ventilators and masks on “our widespread inability to build”. He calls for housing, highly automated factories, nuclear reactors and high-speed trains, and for new ways to deliver education and health care. The problem, he argues, is a lack of desire and will, along with inertia and regulatory capture.

Marc Andreessen, “It's Time to Build”, Andreessen Horowitz (a16z.com), 18 April 2020

2010s

2019Milestone

A new science of progress

Patrick Collison and Tyler Cowen

An entrepreneur and an economist call for a field that studies how progress happens and how to speed it up. By progress they mean economic, technological, scientific, cultural and organisational advance. Such advances, they note, arose in tiny pockets, from Renaissance Florence to Silicon Valley. “We suggest inaugurating the discipline of ‘Progress Studies’,” they write.

Patrick Collison and Tyler Cowen, “We Need a New Science of Progress”, The Atlantic, 30 July 2019; excerpted by Tyler Cowen on Marginal Revolution, 31 July 2019

2012Milestone

Abundance

Peter Diamandis and Steven Kotler

A co-founder of Singularity University and his co-author argue that exponential technologies will soon end scarcity. AI, robotics, digital manufacturing and synthetic biology will let humanity “meet and exceed the basic needs of every person on the planet”. The book takes water, food, energy, health care, education and freedom in turn.

Peter H. Diamandis and Steven Kotler, Abundance: The Future Is Better Than You Think, Free Press, 21 February 2012; publisher's description, Simon & Schuster

VocabularyAbundance

2010Claimed ancestor · cited

What Technology Wants

Kevin Kelly

The founding executive editor of Wired calls the whole system of human technology the technium. It grows almost like a living thing, he argues, with tendencies of its own. Each invention adds options and choices, and that growth of possibility is his definition of progress. Andreessen names Kelly among the patron saints of techno-optimism.

Kevin Kelly, What Technology Wants, Viking, 18 October 2010; Kevin Kelly, “The Positive Balance of Technology”, The Technium, 1 March 2011; Kirkus Reviews, 2010; Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, 2023 (patron saints)

1980s

c. 1980Claimed ancestor · augmentation

A bicycle for the mind

Steve Jobs

In a presentation filmed around 1980, Apple's co-founder compares the personal computer to a bicycle. In a study of how efficiently species move, he recalls, the condor beat humans, but a human on a bicycle beat the condor. A computer, he says in a 1990 film, is “the equivalent of a bicycle for our minds”. Microsoft's Satya Nadella took up the image for AI in 2023 and again in 2025.

Philip Elmer-DeWitt, “Video: Steve Jobs in 1980 on PCs as bicycles for the mind”, Fortune, 14 December 2011; Steve Jobs in Memory & Imagination: New Pathways to the Library of Congress, 1990, excerpted by Maria Popova, The Marginalian, 21 December 2011; Satya Nadella, Build keynote, May 2023, reported by Ben Thompson, Stratechery, 24 May 2023; Satya Nadella, “Looking Ahead to 2026”, 2025

1960s

1962Claimed ancestor · augmentation

“Augmenting Human Intellect”

Douglas Engelbart

In a report for the US Air Force Office of Scientific Research, Engelbart of the Stanford Research Institute sets out a programme. To augment human intellect is “increasing the capability of a man to approach a complex problem situation”. He builds on Vannevar Bush's memex and J. C. R. Licklider's man-computer symbiosis. His lab presented the mouse in 1968, and Andreessen names him a patron saint.

Douglas C. Engelbart, Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework, SRI Summary Report AFOSR-3223, Stanford Research Institute, October 1962 (text at the Doug Engelbart Institute; copy in the Computer History Museum, 102776493); Smithsonian National Museum of American History, replica of Engelbart's first mouse prototype; Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, 2023 (patron saints)

VocabularyAugmentation

1960Attributed by historians · augmentation

“Man-Computer Symbiosis”

J. C. R. Licklider

A psychologist then at Bolt Beranek and Newman hopes that human brains and computers will soon be “coupled together very tightly”. People would set the goals and judge the results, and machines would do the routine work. He grants that machines alone may one day do the thinking, but only after a long interim. Engelbart cites the paper in 1962.

J. C. R. Licklider, “Man-Computer Symbiosis”, IRE Transactions on Human Factors in Electronics HFE-1, March 1960, pp. 4–11; IEEE Computer Society, “Joseph C. R. Licklider”, Computer Pioneers; Douglas C. Engelbart, Augmenting Human Intellect, 1962, section III.A.5; Howard Rheingold, Tools for Thought, Simon & Schuster, 1985, chapter 9

1940s

1945Claimed ancestor · cited

“The Use of Knowledge in Society”

Friedrich Hayek

The Austrian-born economist argues that no central planner can gather the knowledge an economy needs. It exists only as “dispersed bits of incomplete and frequently contradictory knowledge” held by many people. Prices pass it on, like a system of telecommunications. The Techno-Optimist Manifesto invokes Hayek's knowledge problem against any centralised economy.

F. A. Hayek, “The Use of Knowledge in Society”, American Economic Review 35(4), September 1945, pp. 519–530; Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, 2023 (Hayek's Knowledge Problem; patron saints)

1945Attributed by historians · augmentation

“As We May Think”

Vannevar Bush

The magazine introduces Bush as coordinator of some six thousand leading American scientists in the war. He asks what scientists should do next, and answers that the growing store of knowledge must be easier to reach. He imagines the memex, a desk holding a person's books and records, “an enlarged intimate supplement to his memory”. Engelbart's 1962 report devotes a section to the idea.

Vannevar Bush, “As We May Think”, The Atlantic Monthly, July 1945; Douglas C. Engelbart, Augmenting Human Intellect, 1962, sections III.A.1–2; Howard Rheingold, Tools for Thought, Simon & Schuster, 1985, chapter 9

1900s

1909Claimed ancestor · cited

The Manifesto of Futurism

Filippo Tommaso Marinetti

On the front page of Le Figaro, an Italian poet glorifies speed, struggle and war, “the world's only hygiene”. A racing car, he declares, is more beautiful than the Victory of Samothrace. The 2023 Techno-Optimist Manifesto names him a patron saint and adapts his seventh point, putting technology where he wrote poetry. Marinetti went on to co-write the Fascist Manifesto of 1919.

F. T. Marinetti, “The Founding and Manifesto of Futurism”, Le Figaro, 20 February 1909, trans. R. W. Flint, in Marinetti: Selected Writings, 1971 (Encyclopaedia Britannica primary source); Marc Andreessen, “The Techno-Optimist Manifesto”, 2023; Janus Rose, Vice, 17 October 2023

Before 1800

1620Claimed ancestor · cited

Novum Organum

Francis Bacon

England's Lord Chancellor proposes a new method for the sciences, to give people power over nature. “Human knowledge and human power meet in one,” runs his third aphorism, and nature must be obeyed to be commanded. The title page shows a ship sailing past the Pillars of Hercules, the old limit of the known world. Jason Crawford of the progress movement cites him in 2024.

Francis Bacon, Novum Organum, in Instauratio Magna, London, John Bill, 1620, Book I, aphorism III (Internet Modern History Sourcebook, Fordham University); Thomas Fisher Rare Book Library, University of Toronto, on the title page; Jason Crawford, “Why harmony with nature is a myth”, The Techno-Humanist Manifesto, Big Think, 7 August 2024
