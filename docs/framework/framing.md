# A new framing of the AI movements

Nov 25, 2026 · @Thomas Damgaard

## The core insight

The site delivers an insight, not a wiki: every movement in the AI debate answers two old questions, *what should stay human?* and *who should decide?*

Visitors arrive asking whether AI is good or bad. They should leave asking what they want to keep human, and who they trust to decide. ChatGPT felt like year zero. It wasn't, and it was never only about the machine.

- **The personal question, what should stay human:** nothing has to (Transcend); no job or limit is sacred (Accelerate); control (Prevent); work, care, childhood and dignity (Protect); the decision itself (Reclaim); understanding, which the machine lacks (Doubt).
- **The political question, who decides:** markets, the nation, experts, a world authority, the people, the courts, public institutions, workers, or the institutions we already have. That is nearly the whole menu of modern political thought, and every answer is old.

In one line: *tell me what you believe about AI, and I'll tell you who you think should rule.*

## The structure

Six families of two movements each, ordered from the largest claims about what AI will become to the smallest. The headline becomes: *Transcend. Accelerate. Prevent. Protect. Reclaim. Doubt.*

| Family | What it wants | Movements |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Transcend | A future beyond human limits | 01 Transhumanism · 02 Singularitarianism |
| Accelerate | More technology, faster | 03 Techno-optimism · 04 American Dynamism |
| Prevent | To stop AI from ending us | 05 AI safety · 06 Pause |
| Protect | To shield people now, with limits and liability for the builders | 07 Pro-human · 08 AI ethics |
| Reclaim | To change who owns and decides: build our own, or refuse | 09 Digital sovereignty · 10 Neo-Luddism |
| Doubt | To show AI is less than its makers claim | 11 Normal technology · 12 AI skepticism |

**The rules**

- **Family:** what a group wants done, in one verb.
- **Movement:** a whole view of AI (what it is, where it's heading, what to do), called by its own people's name, with founding texts, institutions and vocabulary. Two movements in one family differ in what they value or what they fear.
- **Strand** ("Includes"): a named current that shares its movement's demand and fears, but may ground its values differently. Example: e/acc grounds Techno-optimism's growth in physics rather than human welfare.
- **Order:** each movement sits next to the one it shares most people with, so the grid reads as a chain. Singularitarianism meets Techno-optimism through successionism and e/acc; American Dynamism meets AI safety through Aschenbrenner; Pause meets Pro-human through the Future of Life Institute; AI ethics meets Digital sovereignty through anti-monopoly; Neo-Luddism meets Normal technology in rejecting technological determinism.

**What changes from the current site**

- Risk becomes Prevent. AI safety and Pause become its movements; rationalism and effective altruism become strands.
- e/acc becomes a strand of Techno-optimism, and American Dynamism becomes a movement.
- Doubt is a new family. It is not a critics category: critics stay out of the movement pages, and Doubt holds only those whose whole view is that AI is ordinary or oversold.
- Humanist AI moves to AI safety; neoreaction moves to the timeline and the vocabulary.

**Kept apart on purpose:** American Dynamism and Digital sovereignty share a premise (AI is state power) but take opposite positions on it, the view from the top of the stack and the view from below. Each is the other's sharpest critic.

## The lenses

Every movement page answers the same questions in four lenses, so the critique comes from each movement's own logic, not from its opponents. This replaces the written "Who disputes it" sections. Within a lens, the stated part or the fact comes first, and a bold lead-in marks where the site's own reasoning starts, so no tags are needed.

| Lens | Question it answers | Rule |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Values | What does it want to protect or grow? | Stated by the movement, drawn from a shared list of terms; shown as filled chips in the family colour |
| Fears | What does it dread? | Stated: a fear the movement would admit to; shown as outlined chips |
| Promises | What order of society does it aim at, and what would happen first? | Stated: its own ideal, plus the older tradition it echoes. Then derived, after a bold "If \[movement\]'s demands became law tomorrow,": the first consequences, one step only, in the conditional |
| Interests | Whose money built it, and who could gain from it? | Fact first: public funding records, dated, absence recorded. Then derived, after a bold "Its demands could serve": who stands to gain, who would lose, and the opponents who could gain anyway |
| Includes | Which strands does it hold? | Fact; shown under the name |

**Immanent critique.** Each movement is taken at its word and followed one step. The critique then lives in two gaps the reader notices without the site arguing:

- between what a movement **promises** and what would **happen first** if it won
- between who a movement **says it is for** and who **could see a benefit** in it

**Terms that cut both ways.** The shared terms for values and fears work best when some movements value what others fear: control, speed, the state, going beyond the human, markets, and big claims about AI. These terms also drive the site's features.

**Fairness.** The same test for all twelve, a source behind every line, and equal care for the movements a visitor finds sympathetic.

**Can serve: the rules.** Every idea can be used by someone it was not meant for. The line names which actors could see a benefit in a movement, in its ideas spreading or its demands winning, whether or not they share it.

**Funded by: the rules.** Public records only, dated, with amounts where public. Organisations and named public funders only, never private giving or guesses at motive. Grants to a movement's organisations are kept apart from investment in companies, and where no funding is found, that is recorded too.

**Interests: one lens, a fixed order.** On the page, the funding and who it can serve form one lens, written by hand for each movement as prose, in this order: the funding, who stands to gain, who would lose, the opponents who could gain anyway. It may be the longest lens, as long as the order holds. Funders are named only in the first part and beneficiaries only in the rest, so the page never says a funder gains; when the same name appears in both, the reader sees it. The seven actor types stay as data, in the Can serve grid, for the actor view; they are not listed on the page.

1. **Interest, not motive:** "could see a benefit", never "is behind" or "uses".
2. **A mechanism for each actor** ("since…"), so the reader can check it.
3. **Opponents always checked, and marked.** The most revealing beneficiaries are often the movement's own opponents.
4. **The same actor types and the same rule for all twelve.** A type is left out where no real mechanism exists.

| Actor type | Who |
| --- | --- |
| Big Tech | The largest platforms, frontier labs and chipmakers |
| Challengers | Startups, smaller labs, open-source developers |
| Established industry | Energy, defence contractors, media, finance, manufacturing |
| Patrons | Individuals who fund or found movements and labs with personal fortunes |
| The state | Governments, agencies, militaries |
| Politics | Parties and politicians |
| Institutions | Unions, professions, experts, NGOs, universities |

"Tech oligarchy" is not an actor type: it is a verdict one side of the debate makes, so it belongs in the vocabulary, with who uses it. Patrons are named by role, not wealth: they share a position, not a programme, and fund opposing camps.

## Transcend

The vision of a future beyond human limits. Both movements treat the human as a stage, not an end point.

### 01 Transhumanism

The human condition is not fixed: ageing, illness and the limits of body and mind are problems technology can solve. People should be free to use AI, the strongest tool yet, to become more than human.

- **Values:** self-improvement, longevity, freedom over one's own body
- **Fears:** death, ageing, bans on enhancement
- **Promises:** liberal individualism extended to the body, where each person decides what to become. Echoes Enlightenment perfectibility (Condorcet).
- **If it wins tomorrow:** enhancement becomes a consumer market, and those who can pay go first.
- **Funded by:** tech fortunes. Peter Thiel and Vitalik Buterin funded anti-ageing research, Sam Altman put $180 million into Retro Biosciences, and Altos Labs launched with $3 billion (2022).
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** makers of wearables and health platforms, since self-enhancement runs on constant measurement.
  - **Challengers:** longevity and enhancement startups, since the belief turns ageing into a market.
  - **Established industry:** pharmaceutical and wellness firms, since ageing becomes a condition to treat.
  - **Patrons:** wealthy individuals seeking longer lives, since they can afford enhancement first.
  - **Institutions:** ageing-research labs, since the belief directs money to the biology of ageing.
- **Includes:** longevity and Don't Die, cryonics, extropianism.

### 02 Singularitarianism

Technological change compounds until machine intelligence surpasses ours and history crosses a threshold beyond which it can no longer be predicted. That threshold should be welcomed and prepared for, and some would accept successors that are not human.

- **Values:** intelligence itself, the leap beyond prediction
- **Fears:** standing still; for successionists, humans holding intelligence back
- **Promises:** history with an endpoint, where whoever shapes the transition shapes everything after. Echoes millenarianism.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** everything is staked on reaching AGI first. Capital, energy and talent flow to a few labs, and other priorities wait.
- **Funded by:** the Thiel Foundation (over $1.6 million to the Singularity Institute, now MIRI). Google ($1 million) and other firms sponsored Singularity University at its 2008 launch.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** the labs closest to AGI, since a race rewards whoever is ahead and a near AGI justifies almost any valuation.
  - **Established industry:** energy firms and utilities, since the race needs ever more power.
  - **Patrons:** investors betting on AGI, since the belief turns a long shot into an inevitability.
- **Includes:** successionism.

## Accelerate

The conviction that the answer is more technology, faster. The two movements split on who progress is for: everyone, or the nation.

### 03 Techno-optimism

Technology, markets and building are the engine of progress, and AI will make people richer, healthier and freer. Build it fast, everywhere and for everyone: every delay costs lives.

- **Values:** progress, markets, building; at the e/acc edge, growth as the direction of the universe
- **Fears:** stagnation, precaution, central control
- **Promises:** a market society led by founders, with a light state; at its edge, exit into opt-in network states. Echoes classical liberalism and its libertarian edge.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** rules come off; open models, data centres and automation spread fast. The gains arrive quickly, and so does disruption to work.
- **Funded by:** a few philanthropists. Open Philanthropy and Patrick and John Collison fund progress studies (Institute for Progress, Roots of Progress), and Good Ventures put $60 million into a $120 million Abundance and Growth Fund.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** the largest platforms and labs, since the belief makes any rule look like a cost in lives.
  - **Challengers:** startups and open-source developers, since fewer rules lower the cost of entry.
  - **Established industry:** energy firms, through the data-centre build-out.
  - **Patrons:** founder-investors, since growth flows first to those who own the capital.
  - **Politics:** parties of deregulation, since the belief turns cutting rules into a moral cause.
- **Includes:** effective accelerationism, the network state, progress studies, the abundance agenda, augmentation.

### 04 American Dynamism

AI is the decisive technology of national power, and the race with China will decide who sets the rules. America and its allies must out-build their rivals, with the state and the defence industry as partners.

- **Values:** national strength, building at home, alliances of democracies
- **Fears:** China winning, a hollowed-out industrial base
- **Promises:** a national-security state fused with its tech industry, in a world divided into technology blocs. Echoes Hamiltonian national development and the wartime "arsenal of democracy".
- **If it wins tomorrow:** AI becomes a defence priority; export controls and alliances harden, and the world splits into American and Chinese stacks.
- **Funded by:** venture capital. Andreessen Horowitz raised a $600 million American Dynamism fund (2024), Founders Fund put $1 billion into Anduril (2025), and the Collisons backed Aschenbrenner's AGI fund.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** labs and cloud firms with government contracts, since national projects run through chosen contractors.
  - **Challengers:** defence-tech startups, since the race opens defence budgets to new firms.
  - **Established industry:** defence contractors and energy firms, since rearmament and the build-out need both.
  - **The state:** security establishments on both sides, its rivals included *(opponent)*, since a race justifies budgets in each.
  - **Politics:** China hawks across parties, since the race unites them.
- **Includes:** a national AGI project, AI dominance, the technological republic.

## Prevent

The fear that AI could end us, and the work to stop it. The two movements split on one question: build it carefully, or don't build it at all.

### 05 AI safety

Powerful AI is coming and may become far more capable than we are. The work is to make it aligned, controllable and governed before it outruns us.

- **Values:** control, alignment, careful science
- **Fears:** losing control, a reckless race
- **Promises:** governance by expertise, where scientists, labs and specialist state bodies steer on the public's behalf. Echoes technocracy, from Saint-Simon's society run by savants and engineers.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** frontier AI becomes a licensed industry: a few vetted labs, mandatory tests, and state bodies with the power to halt a model.
- **Funded by:** Open Philanthropy (over $450 million in AI risk grants, including $30 million to OpenAI in 2017) and Jaan Tallinn's Survival and Flourishing Fund ($34 million in 2025). Tallinn also led Anthropic's first big funding round (2021).
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** the frontier labs, since "only responsible builders should build" can become a moat. Contested: Yann LeCun calls it regulatory capture; defenders point out the warnings came before the companies.
  - **The state:** safety institutes and regulators, since licences and tests need them.
  - **Institutions:** the safety-research field and its experts, since governance needs evaluators.
  - **Patrons:** the field's funders, since they gain a say in how AI is governed.
  - *Challengers lose: licences and tests cost most for the smallest players.*
- **Includes:** alignment research, lab safety and responsible scaling, AI governance, effective altruism and longtermism, Humanist AI.

### 06 Pause

Superintelligence built with today's methods would likely end humanity. It should not be built: paused until it can be made safe, or stopped altogether.

- **Values:** human survival, restraint
- **Fears:** extinction, the race itself
- **Promises:** a world order that limits what any state or company may build, the strongest global authority on the map. Echoes postwar arms control, from the Russell–Einstein Manifesto (1955) to the Non-Proliferation Treaty (1968).
- **If it wins tomorrow:** a treaty caps training below today's frontier and tracks chips and data centres. Existing models stay in use, and newcomers get access, not the right to build their own.
- **Funded by:** the Future of Life Institute, endowed by Vitalik Buterin (about $665 million in crypto, 2021) and Elon Musk ($10 million, 2015). It is PauseAI's largest donor (€423,000 of about €715,000).
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** the frontier labs *(opponent)*, since the fear advertises AI as the most powerful technology ever built; and today's leaders, since a freeze keeps their models and blocks newcomers.
  - **The state:** governments seeking oversight of computing, since a treaty requires tracking chips and data centres.
  - **Politics:** politicians on both flanks, since fear of AI unites voters across old lines.
  - **Institutions:** arms-control and safety bodies, since a treaty needs inspectors.
  - *Challengers lose: a cap below the frontier blocks them from building their way up.*
- **Includes:** rationalism, the stop movement, the superintelligence ban.

**Why Pause accepts the lock-in, by its own logic**

1. A monopoly can be broken up later; extinction can't be undone.
2. A freeze stops the leaders too: the race they lead is the thing Pause fears.
3. Few actors make verification possible, as in nuclear arms control.
4. The line must fall as algorithms get more efficient, so draft treaties lower their thresholds over time.
5. Its answer to fairness is access rather than catching up: supervised compute and safe applications for those who join.

Not every proposal locks newcomers out: PauseAI sets its line at GPT-4 level, which anyone may reach. The lock-in comes from treaty drafts that keep existing models in use while capping training below them. That is the pattern of the 1968 Non-Proliferation Treaty, which let those who had the bomb keep it, and which India rejected as "nuclear apartheid".

## Protect

The demand to shield people now from how AI is built and used, through limits and liability for the builders. The two movements split on what most needs protecting: human standing, or human rights.

### 07 Pro-human

AI companies are racing to replace people at work, at home and in power. Humans must stay in charge, with limits on what AI may do, protection for children, and builders who answer for harm.

- **Values:** work, children, dignity, humans in charge
- **Fears:** replacement, manipulative machines, companies that answer to no one
- **Promises:** a human-centred democracy where the people, through elected governments, set the machine's limits, with family, work and community as the things to protect. Echoes Catholic social teaching (Rerum Novarum, 1891) and 1890s American populism.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** limits are written into law (liability, no AI personhood, child and job protections), and adoption slows in homes, schools and workplaces.
- **Funded by:** Frank McCourt ($500 million committed to Project Liberty) and the Future of Life Institute, which convened the Pro-Human Declaration. The Center for Humane Technology got $10 million from 61 grantmakers, among them Omidyar, Open Society and Ford.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** companies that borrow its language *(opponent)*, since "humanist" branding answers the critique without changing the product, as Microsoft's "Humanist AI" code shows.
  - **Established industry:** media, publishing and other industries the limits protect, since a limit protects what already exists.
  - **The state:** national governments, since they write the limits.
  - **Politics:** politicians on both flanks looking for a cause that unites voters.
  - **Institutions:** unions and faith groups, since the coalition gives them a seat at the table.
  - *Challengers lose: liability and limits cost most for small firms launching new products.*
- **Includes:** humane technology, the families' campaigns, faith-based humanism.

### 08 AI ethics

AI already harms people living now, through bias, surveillance, exploited labour and the power of a few companies. Those harms come first, not a future superintelligence, and the systems must answer to the people they affect.

- **Values:** justice, accountability
- **Fears:** bias, surveillance, hidden labour, concentrated corporate power
- **Promises:** a rights-based democracy where courts, regulators and civil society hold systems to account, weighted toward those most exposed. Echoes civil-rights liberalism and critical theory.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** significant systems are audited before and after launch, affected people can contest decisions, and deployment slows while compliance costs rise.
- **Funded by:** older foundations. Ford, MacArthur, Kapor, Open Society and Rockefeller gave DAIR $3.7 million at launch (2021), MacArthur gave AI Now $2 million (2025), and ten foundations pooled $500 million in Humanity AI (2025). Data & Society once also took money from Microsoft.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** firms large enough to absorb compliance, and companies that use principles as reputation *(opponent)*, since audits cost most for the smallest and "ethics-washing" costs little.
  - **Established industry:** consulting and audit firms, since every audit needs an auditor.
  - **The state:** regulators, since accountability runs through them.
  - **Politics:** parties of regulation, since harms happening now are easier to campaign on than harms to come.
  - **Institutions:** lawyers and advocacy NGOs, since rights need people to enforce them.
  - *Challengers lose: compliance costs fall hardest on small firms.*
- **Includes:** algorithmic justice, the ghost-work critique.

## Reclaim

The resolve to change who owns and decides: to build our own, or to refuse. The two movements split on who should hold control: the public, or the citizen where the machine lands.

### 09 Digital sovereignty

A few, mostly American, companies control the chips, clouds, data and models that AI runs on. Countries and their publics should take that stack back and build their own.

- **Values:** independence, public infrastructure
- **Fears:** dependence on a few foreign companies
- **Promises:** a mixed economy where public bodies build and own core infrastructure, in a world of several blocs. Echoes European social democracy and the public-service tradition of post, rail and broadcasting.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** public money builds local chips, clouds and models, and foreign providers face limits. Prices and timelines rise in exchange for independence.
- **Funded by:** states and foundations. The EU's InvestAI aims to mobilise €200 billion (2025), and the Bertelsmann Stiftung commissioned the EuroStack report. Europe's champion Mistral is backed by ASML (€1.3 billion) alongside Nvidia, Andreessen Horowitz and Microsoft.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** chipmakers selling "sovereign AI" *(opponent)*, since local stacks still run on their hardware; Nvidia's CEO has said nations building their own AI will raise demand for his products.
  - **Challengers:** local AI firms, since public money and preference flow to them.
  - **Established industry:** telecoms and national champions, since infrastructure is their business.
  - **The state:** governments and the EU, since building the stack expands their role.
  - **Politics:** nationalists as well as social democrats, since sovereignty speaks to both.
- **Includes:** the anti-monopoly movement, public AI.

### 10 Neo-Luddism

Technology is political: machines carry the interests of those who build them. Workers and citizens, not tech companies, should decide which machines to accept, and they are free to refuse AI.

- **Values:** workers' say, craft, the right to refuse
- **Fears:** deskilling, machines imposed from above
- **Promises:** technology decided where it lands, in the workplace, the town and the classroom. Echoes the Luddites themselves, William Morris and the labour movement.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** workers and communities gain vetoes. AI enters a workplace only by agreement, and adoption becomes uneven, place by place.
- **Funded by:** no major funders found. Its leverage is union contracts, such as the terms on AI the Writers Guild won by strike in 2023.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Institutions:** established professions and strong unions, teachers and academic staff included, since a veto is only as strong as the group holding it.
  - **Established industry:** brands selling the human-made, since refusal creates a premium for the handmade.
  - **Politics:** populists on left and right, since anger at automation mobilises voters.
  - **Challengers:** competitors in places that don't refuse *(opponent)*, since refusal in one place moves the work to another.
- **Includes:** the analogue revival, Marxist neo-Luddism.

## Doubt

The view that AI is less than its makers claim. The two movements split on what is overstated: AI's speed and impact, or its intelligence.

### 11 Normal technology

AI is powerful but normal, like electricity: its effects arrive slowly, through people and institutions, not on their own. Govern its uses with the tools we already have, not for a superintelligence that may never come.

- **Values:** evidence, history, institutions
- **Fears:** policy built on science fiction
- **Promises:** today's institutions, adapting as they did to electricity and the internet. Echoes liberal institutionalism and step-by-step reform.
- **If it wins tomorrow:** existing regulators extend their rules sector by sector, with no emergency powers and no moratoria.
- **Funded by:** no dedicated funding found. Its founding essay was published by the Knight First Amendment Institute, whose supporters include the Knight Foundation, Omidyar and Open Society.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** firms that prefer light rules, since the belief removes the case for emergency powers.
  - **Challengers:** open-source developers, since it argues against non-proliferation rules.
  - **The state:** existing regulators, since governance stays with them.
  - **Institutions:** today's institutions and incumbents, since adapting existing rules favours those the rules already fit.
- **Includes:** the snake-oil critique of predictive AI, the modest-growth economics.

### 12 AI skepticism

Today's AI imitates understanding without having it, and scaling it up will not make a mind. The promises of AGI are hype that serves those selling it, and they should be met with demands for proof.

- **Values:** scientific honesty
- **Fears:** being fooled; money and trust sunk into a bubble
- **Promises:** no new order; attention and money return to real problems. Echoes empiricism, back to Lovelace's objection (1843).
- **If it wins tomorrow:** money and attention pull back and the bubble deflates. The open question is who absorbs the losses.
- **Funded by:** very little. Its best-known podcast runs at DAIR (funded by Ford and MacArthur); other critics publish through books and newsletters.
- **Can serve:**
  - **Big Tech:** the cash-rich incumbents *(opponent)*, since a deflating bubble leaves them standing as rivals fail.
  - **Challengers:** rival research programmes, since doubt about scaling opens room for other methods.
  - **Established industry:** investors betting against the bubble, since a deflating bubble rewards whoever didn't overinvest; and, if it's right, the incumbents AI was meant to disrupt.
  - **Institutions:** independent evaluators and academic critics, since demands for proof need someone to test the claims. When skeptics last won, after the Lighthill report (1973), universities lost funding.
- **Includes:** the hype critique, the neurosymbolic critique, the bubble thesis.

Its timeline is one of the strongest on the map: Lovelace's objection (1843), Dreyfus's critique (1965), the Lighthill report that helped bring on an AI winter (1973), Searle's Chinese Room (1980), then today.

## What the lenses reveal

Read together, the lenses show that the camps argue about the same things from opposite sides, and that almost every belief, followed through, concentrates power somewhere.

1. **One camp's value is another's fear.** Accelerate values speed; Prevent fears the race. Transcend values going beyond the human; Pro-human fears replacement. AI safety values control; Techno-optimism fears it. Techno-optimism values markets; Neo-Luddism and AI ethics fear them.
2. **Everyone fears concentrated power, but whose differs.** e/acc fears regulators, American Dynamism fears China, Pause fears the AI itself, AI ethics and Pro-human fear the companies, Digital sovereignty fears American companies, Neo-Luddism fears bosses and capital, Doubt fears the hype machine.
3. **Urgency drives everything.** The more extraordinary a movement believes AI is, the more exceptional the measures its belief justifies: race-scale capital, a national-security state, expert custodians, a global authority. Movements that see AI as normal enable ordinary politics.
4. **The power paradox.** Followed through, most beliefs concentrate power: in labs, the security state, experts, a treaty body, elected states, regulators or public bodies. Neo-Luddism pushes decisions down to workplaces and towns. Doubt concentrates nothing, and leaves us least prepared if it is wrong.
5. **One story covers opposite powers.** Doom talk serves the labs, by making their product sound world-changing, and their opponents, by justifying regulation. Lee Vinsel calls criticism that inflates the hype "criti-hype". The China race helps labs argue against rules and defence ministries argue for budgets.
6. **Language gets borrowed by the power it opposes.** Nvidia sells "sovereign AI" to governments; Microsoft's "Humanist AI" code borrows pro-human language; frontier labs call to "pace the frontier".
7. **Every virtue has its washing:** ethics-washing, safety-washing, open-washing, AI-washing. Each is the visible trace of a belief used as cover, and together they make a natural vocabulary set.
8. **Each family fears a different time.** Transcend and Accelerate fear standing still; Prevent fears the future; Protect and Reclaim fear the present; Doubt fears a mistake of judgement.

**From the actor view.** Read across all twelve "Can serve" lines, four patterns appear. They come from the full grid in the second tab, a first draft that must be checked with sources before publishing.

- **Power is a position, not a camp.** Big Tech could gain under 10 of the 12 movements, in 4 of them (Pause, Pro-human, AI ethics, Digital sovereignty) as the movement's own opponent. Even AI skepticism could leave it stronger, since a burst bubble leaves the cash-rich standing as rivals fail.
- **Some actors rarely gain.** Challengers lose under every movement that adds rules: AI safety, Pause, Pro-human and AI ethics. Workers outside unions and professions appear nowhere as beneficiaries, even under the movements that speak for them.
- **Patrons gain only from the big claims.** They could gain under all seven movements from Transhumanism to Pro-human, and under none of the five from AI ethics to AI skepticism.
- **Patrons fund both the alarm and the engine.** [Jaan Tallinn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaan_Tallinn) co-founded the Future of Life Institute and was an early investor in DeepMind and Anthropic. [Peter Thiel's foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thiel_Foundation) gave over $1.6 million to MIRI; he was also DeepMind's first investor and is now a critic of effective altruism. [Elon Musk gave $10 million to FLI](https://futureoflife.org/2015/01/22/elon-musk-donates-10m-to-our-research-program/) in 2015, the year he co-founded OpenAI. Much of the fight is funded from a small circle.

## The politics forming around AI

A hypothesis to test, not a prediction: AI may be drawing new political lines across left and right, as the first industrial revolution drew the old ones.

**The signs**

- The [Pro-Human AI Declaration](https://www.nbcnews.com/tech/tech-news/-human-ai-declaration-brings-together-unlikely-group-calling-trustwort-rcna261594) (March 2026) was signed by Steve Bannon, Glenn Beck, Ralph Nader, Susan Rice and Daron Acemoglu, among others.
- At the [Pro-Human Assembly](https://www.npr.org/2026/09/15/nx-s1-5968678/bernie-sanders-and-steve-bannon-and-ai) (September 2026), Sanders and Bannon shared a stage, and more than 50 organisations launched a coalition.
- One essay already names the moment ["AI populism"](https://press.princeton.edu/node/71315), and AI voting in Congress [does not yet follow party lines](https://www.newsweek.com/how-ai-upended-politics-and-left-lawmakers-scrambling-to-pick-a-side-12501075).

**The rhyme.** [Pope Leo XIV took his name](https://www.nbcnews.com/world/the-vatican/pope-leo-ai-played-factor-papal-name-rcna206126) from Leo XIII, whose Rerum Novarum (1891) answered the first industrial revolution. That machine age produced the politics of labour and capital. The question is what this one produces.

**Lines visible in the movements**

- Keep the human, or surpass it
- Speed, or control
- Who owns the machine: concentrated, or distributed
- For whom: the nation, everyone, the citizen, or the public

**Cautions**

- Some analysts argue AI will [activate familiar divides rather than invent new ones](https://kylesaunders.substack.com/p/artificial-intelligence-and-politics). The site should put the question to visitors, not announce an answer.
- The evidence is mostly American. A global claim needs Europe, China, the Global South and the Vatican.
- Pro-human fits the thesis best, so the site must take care not to read as its advocate.

**The data.** One file: 12 movements by about 10 shared terms, each cell marked as a value, a fear or neither, plus every "Can serve" actor tagged by type and marked when it is the movement's opponent, and each movement's funders with amount, date and source. Every feature below runs from it.

**Features**

1. **Highlight.** Pick a term and the grid splits: movements that value it light up in their highlighter colour, those that fear it are outlined, the rest fade.
2. **Same fear, different answers.** Pick "concentrated power" and see whose power each movement fears.
3. **Make it win.** Pick a movement and see the world the next morning, built from its own published proposals. In the exhibition, a room.
4. **The actor view.** Pick an actor type, such as Big Tech, Challengers or Patrons, and see every movement that could serve it, with opponents marked.
5. **The patron view.** A handful of patrons, with lines to the movements and labs they founded or funded. One person connected to opposing camps needs no commentary.
6. **The age of a fear.** Follow one fear through the timeline: replacement, from the frame-breakers of 1812 through Butler in 1863 to Pro-human in 2026.
7. **The dated map.** Each new version records how the camps move.

**Fairness rules**

- A source behind every value, fear, consequence and beneficiary.
- The same test and the same actor types for all twelve movements.
- Fears a movement would admit to; consequences one step from its own demands.
- "Could see a benefit", never "is behind"; a mechanism for every actor.
- Patrons: only public, dated funding facts, and nothing about motives.
