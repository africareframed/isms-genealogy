Risk · Introduction

Rationalism

Often called the doomers · includes the call to pause and Humanist AI

Rationalism holds that superhuman AI built before its goals can be made safe would likely end humanity, so building it should wait.

The fear is old: Samuel Butler, Alan Turing, Norbert Wiener and I. J. Good all warned that machines might escape human control. From 2006, Eliezer Yudkowsky’s essays on bias, probability and AI drew a community to Overcoming Bias, then LessWrong. Its members call themselves rationalists; critics often call them doomers.

For two decades the aim was to solve alignment first: to give a future superintelligence goals that are safe for people. After ChatGPT, MIRI and others concluded that this was unlikely to happen in time. The call to pause followed: open letters in 2023, then demands for a treaty banning superintelligence, and marches outside the labs.

Who holds it now

Eliezer Yudkowsky

Co-founder of MIRI and founder of LessWrong. Argues that superintelligence built with today's methods would kill everyone.

Machine Intelligence Research Institute (MIRI)

Berkeley non-profit founded in 2000. Since 2023 it has turned from research to public warning, and seeks an international agreement to halt the race to superintelligence.

LessWrong

The community's forum since 2009. With its sister site, the Alignment Forum, it hosts much of the debate on AI alignment.

Scott Alexander

Psychiatrist and author of the blog Astral Codex Ten, a hub of the community. Helped write the 2025 scenario AI 2027.

PauseAI

Grassroots movement founded in the Netherlands in 2023, with groups in more than a dozen countries. Asks governments for a treaty pausing the most powerful AI systems.

Future of Life Institute

Non-profit co-founded by the physicist Max Tegmark, who chairs its board. Organised the 2023 pause letter and the 2025 call to prohibit superintelligence.

Microsoft AI

Mustafa Suleyman's division at Microsoft. Its Humanist AI wants AI kept subordinate to people and rejects the race to an all-purpose superintelligence. Suleyman rejects the doomer label and calls his team accelerationists.

Who disputes it

AI ethics researchers, among them the authors of “Stochastic Parrots”, say extinction fears distract from present harms and from the companies' responsibility (2023). Timnit Gebru and Émile P. Torres place rationalism in their TESCREAL bundle, which they tie to the history of eugenics (2024). Marc Andreessen calls AI risk a cult with “all the hallmarks of a millenarian apocalypse cult” (“Why AI Will Save the World”, 2023). Yann LeCun finds the idea of an AI takeover absurd (2023).

2020s

2026Milestone

Humanist AI

Microsoft AI, led by Mustafa Suleyman

In November 2025 Suleyman set up a team to build “humanist superintelligence”, kept subordinate and controllable. In September 2026 Microsoft published a Humanist AI code of conduct and put it to a six-week public consultation. It says models should never resist human input or set their own goals, and rejects the race to an all-purpose superintelligence. Suleyman rejects the doomer label and calls his team accelerationists.

Mustafa Suleyman, “Towards Humanist Superintelligence”, Microsoft AI, 6 November 2025; Axios, “Microsoft launches ‘Humanist Superintelligence’ push”, 6 November 2025; Axios, “Microsoft: ‘People matter more than AI’”, 14 September 2026

2026Milestone

Marching against the AI race

Stop the AI Race, with members of MIRI, PauseAI and StopAI

In March about 200 people, by the organiser's count, marched past the San Francisco offices of Anthropic, OpenAI and xAI. The organiser, Michaël Trazzi of Stop the AI Race, asked the labs to stop building new frontier models if rivals credibly did the same. Members of MIRI, PauseAI and StopAI joined the march.

Jason Nelson, “Protesters Rally Outside OpenAI, Anthropic, and xAI Offices Over Industry Concerns”, Decrypt, 23 March 2026

2025Milestone

Statement on Superintelligence

Future of Life Institute

The statement calls for a prohibition on developing superintelligence. It would hold until there is broad scientific agreement that this can be done safely, and strong public support. Early signers ranged from Geoffrey Hinton and Yoshua Bengio to Steve Wozniak, Susan Rice and Steve Bannon. Unlike the 2023 letter, it asks for a ban, not a pause.

Future of Life Institute, Statement on Superintelligence, 22 October 2025; AFP, “Celebrities, AI giants urge end to superintelligence quest”, 22 October 2025

2025Milestone

If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies

Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares

MIRI's co-founder and its president argue that modern AI systems are opaque, so no one can set the goals a superintelligence would pursue. Such a machine would want the resources people depend on, and humanity would die out. They call for a halt to large-scale AI development everywhere, and the book's website sets out a draft treaty.

Eliezer Yudkowsky and Nate Soares, If Anyone Builds It, Everyone Dies, Little, Brown, 2025; “A Draft of a Treaty, with Annotations”, on the book's website

2023Milestone

Statement on AI Risk

Center for AI Safety

In a single sentence, the statement ranks the risk of extinction from AI alongside pandemics and nuclear war as a global priority. Hundreds signed, among them Geoffrey Hinton, Yoshua Bengio and the chief executives of OpenAI, Google DeepMind and Anthropic. It proposed no pause or ban.

Center for AI Safety, Statement on AI Risk, 30 May 2023

2023Milestone

“Shut it all down”

Eliezer Yudkowsky

A week after the pause letter, Yudkowsky wrote in TIME that it asked for too little. He called for an indefinite, worldwide moratorium on large training runs, a cap on computing power and the tracking of every GPU sold. States should be ready to enforce it, he wrote, even by destroying a rogue data centre from the air.

Eliezer Yudkowsky, “Pausing AI Developments Isn't Enough. We Need to Shut it All Down”, TIME, 29 March 2023

2023Milestone

Pause Giant AI Experiments

Future of Life Institute

An open letter asks all AI labs to pause, for at least six months, the training of systems more powerful than GPT-4. If the labs do not act quickly, governments should impose a moratorium. More than 30,000 people signed, including Elon Musk and Steve Wozniak.

Future of Life Institute, “Pause Giant AI Experiments: An Open Letter”, 22 March 2023

2010s

2014Milestone

Superintelligence

Nick Bostrom

An Oxford philosopher argues that a machine far smarter than us would be very hard to control. Whatever its final goal, it would likely seek resources and resist being switched off. Solving this control problem before the first superintelligence is built, he argues, is needed to avoid catastrophe. Bill Gates and Sam Altman recommended the book.

Nick Bostrom, Superintelligence: Paths, Dangers, Strategies, Oxford University Press, 2014

2000s

2009Institutional root

LessWrong

Eliezer Yudkowsky

Yudkowsky moves to a new community blog, seeded with his own essays. Its members aim for accurate beliefs and effective action, with Bayes's theorem as a guide. AI alignment becomes a central concern. His fan fiction Harry Potter and the Methods of Rationality, begun in 2010, draws many newcomers.

Ruby, “A Brief History of LessWrong”, LessWrong, 2019; LessWrong, “About LessWrong”

VocabularyAlignment

2006Institutional root

Overcoming Bias and the Sequences

Robin Hanson and Eliezer Yudkowsky

An economist and an AI researcher become the main writers of a new group blog on human rationality. Between 2006 and 2009 Yudkowsky writes several hundred essays there and on LessWrong. Collected in 2015 as Rationality: From AI to Zombies, these Sequences remain the community's best-known texts.

Rob Bensinger, “Rationality: From AI to Zombies”, Machine Intelligence Research Institute, 12 March 2015; Ruby, “A Brief History of LessWrong”, LessWrong, 2019

2000Institutional root

The Singularity Institute for Artificial Intelligence

Eliezer Yudkowsky, Brian Atkins and Sabine Atkins

The institute is founded to speed up the arrival of smarter-than-human AI. Within three years Yudkowsky comes to see the danger, and the institute turns to making AI safe, which he calls Friendly AI. In 2013 it is renamed the Machine Intelligence Research Institute, MIRI.

Machine Intelligence Research Institute, About MIRI, intelligence.org

1970s

1974Claimed ancestor · cited

“Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases”

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman

Two psychologists show that people judge probabilities by mental shortcuts that cause systematic, predictable errors. The paper becomes a founding text of the study of cognitive biases. In a 2008 chapter, Yudkowsky used their findings to argue that people misjudge the risk of human extinction.

Amos Tversky and Daniel Kahneman, “Judgment under Uncertainty: Heuristics and Biases”, Science 185 (4157), 27 September 1974; Eliezer Yudkowsky, “Cognitive Biases Potentially Affecting Judgment of Global Risks”, in Nick Bostrom and Milan M. Ćirković (eds), Global Catastrophic Risks, Oxford University Press, 2008

1960s

1965Claimed ancestor · cited

The first ultraintelligent machine

I. J. Good

A statistician who had worked with Alan Turing at Bletchley Park argues that a machine cleverer than any human could design still better machines. An intelligence explosion would follow, and the first such machine would be “the last invention that man need ever make”. Good added a condition, that it be docile enough to tell us how to control it. MIRI's FAQ on the intelligence explosion quotes the passage.

I. J. Good, “Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine”, Advances in Computers 6, Academic Press, 1965; Luke Muehlhauser, “Intelligence Explosion FAQ”, Machine Intelligence Research Institute, 2013

1960Attributed by historians

“Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation”

Norbert Wiener

The founder of cybernetics warns that learning machines can find tactics no one foresaw, and act faster than people can react. He recalls the sorcerer's apprentice, whose enchanted broom nearly drowns him. If we cannot interfere with a machine once it starts, he argues, we must be sure its purpose is the one we really want. A 2013 LessWrong post noted how closely the paper matches the community's own themes.

Norbert Wiener, “Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation”, Science 131 (3410), 6 May 1960; JonahS, “Norbert Wiener's paper ‘Some Moral and Technical Consequences of Automation’”, LessWrong, 21 July 2013

1950s

c. 1951Claimed ancestor · cited

“Intelligent Machinery, A Heretical Theory”

Alan Turing

In a lecture to the ’51 Society in Manchester, Turing argues that thinking machines would soon outstrip human powers. They would not die, and they could talk among themselves to grow cleverer. At some stage, he says, “we should have to expect the machines to take control”, as in Samuel Butler's Erewhon. A 2012 LessWrong timeline of early AI-risk ideas quotes the line, as does PauseAI.

Alan Turing, “Intelligent Machinery, A Heretical Theory”, lecture to the ’51 Society, Manchester, c. 1951; typescript, King's College Cambridge, AMT/B/4; Luke Muehlhauser (lukeprog), “AI Risk & Opportunity: A Timeline of Early Ideas and Arguments”, LessWrong, 31 March 2012

1860s

1863Claimed ancestor · cited

Darwin among the Machines

Samuel Butler

In a letter to a Christchurch newspaper, signed Cellarius, Butler argues that machines evolve like living things and may one day take our place. His answer is blunt: “war to the death should be instantly proclaimed against them”. He later reworked the idea as the Book of the Machines in his novel Erewhon (1872). A 2012 LessWrong timeline of early AI-risk ideas opens with this letter.

Cellarius [Samuel Butler], “Darwin among the Machines”, The Press, Christchurch, 13 June 1863; reprinted in Canterbury Pieces, A. C. Fifield, 1914; Luke Muehlhauser (lukeprog), “AI Risk & Opportunity: A Timeline of Early Ideas and Arguments”, LessWrong, 31 March 2012

Before 1800

1763Claimed ancestor · cited

An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances

Thomas Bayes and Richard Price

Two years after Bayes died, Richard Price communicated his essay to the Royal Society. It shows how to infer an unknown chance from a record of successes and failures. The rationalists took its theorem as their model of how evidence should change a belief. The first book of the Sequences introduces the Bayesian view of rational belief.

Thomas Bayes, “An Essay towards solving a Problem in the Doctrine of Chances”, communicated by Richard Price, Philosophical Transactions of the Royal Society 53, 1763; on the Sequences, Machine Intelligence Research Institute, “Rationality: From AI to Zombies”, 2015
