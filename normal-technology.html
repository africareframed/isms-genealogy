Normal technology

Includes the snake-oil critique of predictive AI and the modest-growth economics

Normal technology holds that AI is powerful but ordinary, and that its uses should be governed with the tools we already have.

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor see AI as transformative but normal, like electricity, spreading over decades. On that view both superintelligence fears and utopian hype are misplaced.

Economists supply its history. Electricity took decades to show up in productivity, and in 1987 Robert Solow saw the computer age everywhere but in the statistics. In 2024 Daron Acemoglu put AI’s boost to productivity over ten years at well under one per cent.

Who holds it now

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor

Computer scientists from Princeton who wrote AI Snake Oil (2024). Their newsletter, AI as Normal Technology, treats AI as transformative but normal, not as a coming superintelligence.

Daron Acemoglu

MIT economist and Nobel laureate (2024). He expects AI to raise productivity far less than its makers say, because few tasks can be automated profitably soon.

Robert J. Gordon

Northwestern economist. He argues that today's technologies, computers and AI among them, have not matched the growth that electricity and the car once brought.

Erik Brynjolfsson

Stanford economist. He treats AI as a general-purpose technology, like electricity, whose gains come only after firms reorganise around it.

Promises

Today’s institutions, adapting as they did to electricity and the internet. It echoes liberal institutionalism and reform step by step.

If Normal technology’s demands became law tomorrow, existing regulators would extend their rules sector by sector, with no emergency powers and no moratoria.

Interests

No dedicated funding found. Its founding essay was published in April 2025 by the Knight First Amendment Institute at Columbia University, whose major supporters include the Knight Foundation, the Omidyar Group, Open Society and the Hewlett Foundation.

Its demands could serve those already in charge of the rules. Existing regulators gain, since governance stays with them, and today’s institutions gain, since adapting existing rules favours those the rules already fit. Established firms adopting AI at their own pace gain too, since diffusion is slow and runs through them.

Firms that prefer light rules could gain, since the belief removes the case for emergency powers. Open-source developers gain as well, since it argues against rules to stop AI spreading.

No actor type stands to lose outright. Parties and patrons have little to take from it: it removes the urgency that mobilises voters, and ordinary governance gives fortunes no special stake.

AI as Normal Technology

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor

Narayanan and Kapoor argue that AI is a normal technology, like electricity or the internet, transformative but a tool people can control. New methods may come fast, they write, but adoption takes decades, as electrification did. They doubt that superintelligence, as usually framed, is a coherent idea, and put resilience rather than nonproliferation at the centre of policy.

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, “AI as Normal Technology”, Knight First Amendment Institute at Columbia University, 15 April 2025

2024Milestone

AI Snake Oil

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor

Two Princeton computer scientists use the name for AI products that do not work and probably never will. They describe harms AI already causes in education, medicine, hiring, banking, insurance and criminal justice. They grant the potential of some AI, such as ChatGPT, but warn against control by largely unaccountable big tech companies. What people will do with AI worries them far more than AI acting on its own.

Arvind Narayanan and Sayash Kapoor, AI Snake Oil: What Artificial Intelligence Can Do, What It Can't, and How to Tell the Difference, Princeton University Press, 24 September 2024 (publisher's description)

The Simple Macroeconomics of AI

Daron Acemoglu

The MIT economist estimates what AI can add to productivity over the next ten years, based on the tasks it can take over profitably. His answer is a gain of well under one per cent in total factor productivity. The paper became a reference for those who expect modest growth from AI.

Daron Acemoglu, “The Simple Macroeconomics of AI”, NBER Working Paper 32487, May 2024

Against Predictive Optimization

Angelina Wang, Sayash Kapoor, Solon Barocas and Arvind Narayanan

Four researchers examine systems that decide about people by predicting their future, from welfare to hiring and criminal justice. They argue that such systems share recurring flaws, from social outcomes that cannot be predicted well to training data that do not match where the systems are used.

Angelina Wang, Sayash Kapoor, Solon Barocas and Arvind Narayanan, “Against Predictive Optimization”, ACM Conference on Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency (FAccT), 2023

The productivity J-curve

Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock and Chad Syverson

Three economists explain why general-purpose technologies first seem to lower productivity before raising it. Firms must invest in new skills, processes and organisation that the statistics do not count. On this view AI's gains arrive late, as electricity's did.

Erik Brynjolfsson, Daniel Rock and Chad Syverson, “The Productivity J-Curve. How Intangibles Complement General Purpose Technologies”, American Economic Journal. Macroeconomics 13(1), 2021

How to recognize AI snake oil

Arvind Narayanan

In a talk at MIT, the Princeton computer scientist separates AI that works, such as image recognition, from AI that cannot, such as software claiming to predict who will reoffend or succeed in a job. He calls the second kind snake oil. The talk led to the book.

Arvind Narayanan, “How to recognize AI snake oil”, talk slides, MIT, November 2019

The Rise and Fall of American Growth

Robert J. Gordon

The Northwestern economist argues that the great inventions of 1870 to 1970, from electricity to the car, raised living standards in ways that newer technologies have not matched. Computers, he finds, gave only a short burst of faster growth.

Robert J. Gordon, The Rise and Fall of American Growth, Princeton University Press, 2016

Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital

Carlota Perez

The economist describes how each great technology, from railways to computers, arrives in waves. A speculative frenzy is followed by a crash, and only then by a long period in which the technology spreads through the economy.

Carlota Perez, Technological Revolutions and Financial Capital, Edward Elgar, 2002

The Dynamo and the Computer

Paul A. David

The Stanford economic historian compares computers with the electric dynamo. Factories took decades to reorganise around electric power before productivity rose, he shows, and computers may follow the same slow path.

Paul A. David, “The Dynamo and the Computer. An Historical Perspective on the Modern Productivity Paradox”, American Economic Review 80(2), 1990

The productivity paradox

Robert Solow

Reviewing a book on manufacturing, the Nobel economist writes that “you can see the computer age everywhere but in the productivity statistics”. The line named a puzzle that returns with every new technology, AI included.

Robert M. Solow, “We'd Better Watch Out”, New York Times Book Review, 12 July 1987

Diffusion of Innovations

Everett M. Rogers

The rural sociologist describes how new ideas and tools spread through a society, from early adopters to laggards, along an S-shaped curve. Diffusion, he shows, depends on people and institutions as much as on the invention.

Everett M. Rogers, Diffusion of Innovations, Free Press, 1962
