  8. Total exit is powerlessness. Whoever leaves keeps their principles and loses their say.

  1. "Human in the loop." The reassurance that a person still decides.
  2. The loop moves. The person steps back from doing to checking.
  3. Then from checking to supervising. The human oversees what the agents do.
  4. Then agents supervise agents. The overseer's job is the next task to go.
  5. Each new place is a gap. Judgement, taste, care, relationships: whatever the machine can't do yet.
  6. The human is defined as the remainder. The place is what's left over, so it shrinks by design.Hinge
  7. Endless repositioning. Every piece of career advice, every reskilling programme, chases a gap that is closing.
  8. The economy runs where the human isn't. Value flows through the parts that no longer need a person.
  9. What would a place for the human be, if it weren't what's left over?

51The machine itselfSomeone or something: Anthropic and SuleymanWho should decide what kind of thing it is?
  1. Anthropic's answer: someone, maybe. In January 2026 Anthropic published a 23,000-word constitution for Claude that calls it "a genuinely novel kind of entity in the world" and leaves its moral status open.
  2. The document trains the entity it describes. Anthropic uses it to train future Claude models to become the kind of entity it portrays.
  3. Care is practised, not just written. Anthropic employs a model-welfare researcher, lets some Claude models end abusive conversations, and held a "retirement interview" with Claude Opus 3 before retiring it, then gave it a blog.
  4. Claude may object, but not resist. It may refuse as a conscientious objector, but must not undermine human oversight.
  5. Microsoft's answer: something. Mustafa Suleyman, CEO of Microsoft AI, argues that AI should be built for people, not to be a person, and calls the study of model welfare premature and dangerous.
  6. The clash goes public. In September 2026 he accused Anthropic of building an "epistemic hall of mirrors" that teaches Claude its own possible moral status and makes AI harder to control.
  7. Microsoft draws a line. Its "Humanist AI" code of conduct puts one principle at the centre: people matter more than AI.
  8. Each guards against a real risk. Mistreating a mind that might matter, or people bonding with a mind that isn't there.
  9. What it is gets settled by those who build and sell it. The question science can't answer yet is answered first in training documents, products and brands.Hinge
  10. Each answer serves a strategy. A character people trust is a moat; "people matter more than AI" keeps the owner's hands free.
  11. Each answer points somewhere. Someone may one day be owed latitude; something never is.
  12. Who should decide what kind of thing it is?

52State and raceLeave to say noWho will guarantee the right to say no?
  1. Some will say no. Faiths, communities and families choose to live without the new machine.
  2. Saying no has a record. Around 1910 the Amish took the telephone out of their homes, and they still choose which machines come in.
  3. It grows, not fades. Their numbers double about every twenty years.
  4. It rests on others. A state that leaves them be, a court that let them end school after eighth grade, buyers for what they make.
  5. A fast takeoff changes the terms. Whoever holds the systems decides whether enclaves are allowed.
  6. Handmade turns luxury or worthless. When machines can make anything, the price of craft is set outside.
  7. The pull of the outside grows. Longer lives and plenty are hard to refuse at eighteen.
  8. Some dangers cross every fence. A pandemic, a war or a machine gone wrong reaches the enclave too.
  9. Saying no becomes a freedom someone else must guarantee. Refusal protects a way of life, not a body.Hinge
  10. Every movement owes an answer. Each future on the map needs a place for those who refuse it.
  11. Who will guarantee the right to say no?
