lead: Self-determination asks who decides how AI uses people's data and shapes their future, and the isms split between voice, markets, personal choice and exit.
isms:
  - ism: transhumanism
    text: Self-determination starts with one's own body and mind, which each person should be free to change. The Transhumanist Declaration favours “wide personal choice”, from memory aids and life extension to cryonics.
  - ism: singularitarianism
    text: Sam Altman wants superintelligence “cheap, widely available, and not too concentrated”, with user freedom inside bounds that society decides. Richard Sutton doubts anyone can steer it, since humanity has no single dominant view.
  - ism: rationalism
    text: It asks that the public, not the labs alone, decide whether superintelligence is built. The Future of Life Institute's 2025 statement seeks a ban until there is broad scientific agreement and strong public support.
  - ism: effective-altruism
    text: Its longtermists fear that AI could lock in one set of values for a very long time, deciding for every generation to come. William MacAskill makes the warning in What We Owe the Future (2022).
  - ism: effective-accelerationism
    text: "Self-determination means exit: leaving a state instead of reforming it. Balaji Srinivasan's network state would crowdfund territory and seek recognition as a new country."
  - ism: techno-optimism
    text: Markets, not planners, should decide, since knowledge is spread among many people, as Hayek argued. Reid Hoffman's bloomers want AI in many hands, shaped by its users' feedback.
strands:
  - name: Indigenous data sovereignty
    text: "In 2019 the International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group set out the CARE Principles: collective benefit, authority to control, responsibility and ethics. They add a focus on people and purpose to the data-centred FAIR principles of open science. Their aim is that users of Indigenous data “Be FAIR and CARE”."
    source: International Indigenous Data Sovereignty Interest Group, Research Data Alliance, CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance, 2019; Stephanie Russo Carroll et al., “The CARE Principles for Indigenous Data Governance”, Data Science Journal 19(1), 2020
  - name: Māori data sovereignty
    text: Te Hiku Media, a Māori non-profit broadcaster in New Zealand, built speech-to-text tools for te reo Māori from recordings by native speakers. Partners must accept its Kaitiakitanga licence, which keeps the data with local communities and bars uses that surveil or discriminate. “Data is the new land,” said its chief executive, Peter-Lucas Jones, in 2022.
    source: ITU News, “How AI is helping revitalise indigenous languages”, 9 August 2022
  - name: Plurality
    text: Audrey Tang, Taiwan's first digital minister, and the economist Glen Weyl argue that digital tools can strengthen democracy across social differences. Their 2024 book charts a path “between the extremes of techno-libertarianism and centralized AI governance”. Written with a community of contributors, it is dedicated to the public domain.
    source: E. Glen Weyl, Audrey Tang and the Plurality Community, Plurality, 2024, plurality.net
  - name: Citizens' assemblies
    text: Panels of randomly selected citizens learn about AI, deliberate and make recommendations. Sixty Belgians met for their country's EU presidency in 2024 and asked for “every human in the loop”; forty Swiss followed in 2025. In 2023 Anthropic and the Collective Intelligence Project trained a model on a constitution shaped by 1,000 Americans.
    source: Belgian Citizens' Panel on AI, report launched 25 May 2024 (Connected by Data); EPFL AI Center, Citizens' Assembly on AI, November 2025; Collective Intelligence Project, “CIP and Anthropic launch Collective Constitutional AI”, 17 October 2023
  - name: Exit and the network state
    text: "Balaji Srinivasan's The Network State (2022) proposes online communities that crowdfund territory and win recognition as new countries. It builds on the idea of exit, leaving a system instead of reforming it. Divya Siddarth, Glen Weyl and Anne-Marie Slaughter reply: “Exit is a necessary right, but it requires something to exit to.”"
    source: Balaji Srinivasan, The Network State, 2022, thenetworkstate.com; Divya Siddarth, Glen Weyl and Anne-Marie Slaughter, “We need network societies, not network states”, Collective Intelligence Project

The demand comes from many directions. Indigenous peoples claim authority over data about their lands, languages and communities. Democratic reformers want citizens, not only companies, to set the rules for AI.

The isms give their own answers. Transhumanists stress personal choice over body and mind, accelerationists the right to exit, and rationalists a treaty with public backing.
