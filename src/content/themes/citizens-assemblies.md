Members are drawn at random, then balanced by traits such as age, gender and region. They hear experts, deliberate and vote on recommendations, which are usually advisory. For AI, assemblies are proposed as a way to give the public a say in decisions now taken by a few.

Choosing by lot is ancient: in Athens, allotment machines called kleroteria picked jurors and officials. Modern versions began in the 1970s, with Ned Crosby's citizens' juries in the United States and Peter Dienel's planning cells in Germany. In 2004 an assembly of 160 randomly selected British Columbians studied the province's voting system.

Such bodies have multiplied since 2010: an OECD study of 2020 drew on 289 cases. The idea entered the AI debate as labs looked for public input. In May 2023 OpenAI offered ten grants of $100,000 for experiments in deciding, democratically, what rules AI should follow.
