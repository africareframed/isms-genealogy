isms:
  - ism: transhumanism
    text: Growth in computing power is what brings its goals within reach. Ray Kurzweil's 2024 book expects human-level AI by 2029, and minds extended into the cloud by 2045.
  - ism: singularitarianism
    text: Scale is its engine. Kurzweil's law of accelerating returns treats Moore's law as only the fifth in a series of exponential paradigms of computing.
  - ism: rationalism
    text: Scale brings the danger closer, so scale is what to cap. Eliezer Yudkowsky called in 2023 for a limit on computing power and the tracking of every GPU sold.
  - ism: effective-altruism
    text: Effective altruists have tried to forecast AI from the computing power it would need. Ajeya Cotra's 2020 draft report for Open Philanthropy anchored its estimates on biology, from the human brain to evolution. Its median for transformative AI was about 2050.
  - ism: effective-accelerationism
    text: More energy and more computation are goals in themselves. The e/acc newsletter aims to climb “the Kardashev gradient”, and Extropic hopes its chips will lift energy limits on AI scaling.
  - ism: techno-optimism
    text: Growth is the aim, and building is the means. Marc Andreessen's 2023 manifesto would put intelligence and energy in a positive feedback loop and “drive them both to infinity”.
strands:
  - name: The bitter lesson
    text: Richard Sutton's 2019 essay draws one lesson from seventy years of AI, that “general methods that leverage computation are ultimately the most effective”. In chess, Go, speech and vision, he argues, search and learning at scale beat methods built on human knowledge.
    source: Rich Sutton, “The Bitter Lesson”, incompleteideas.net, 13 March 2019
  - name: Scaling laws
    text: In 2020 Jared Kaplan and colleagues at OpenAI found that a language model's error falls predictably as model size, data and compute grow. Details of design, such as the width or depth of the network, mattered little by comparison. Larger models also learned more from each example.
    source: Jared Kaplan, Sam McCandlish and others, “Scaling Laws for Neural Language Models”, arXiv:2001.08361, 23 January 2020
  - name: Compute thresholds
    text: Some laws measure an AI model by the computing power used to train it. The EU's AI Act presumes that a general-purpose model trained with more than 10²⁵ floating-point operations has high-impact capabilities. Such a model is classed as carrying systemic risk. The European Commission can change that threshold as the technology moves.
    source: Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the AI Act), Article 51, Official Journal of the European Union, 12 July 2024
  - name: Energy and data centres
    text: Scale now means buildings, chips and power lines. In April 2025 the International Energy Agency projected that data centres would use about 945 terawatt-hours by 2030, up from 415 in 2024. That is slightly more than Japan uses today, and AI is the main driver.
    source: International Energy Agency, Energy and AI, 10 April 2025, as reported by Sophia Chen, “Data Centers Will Use Twice as Much Energy by 2030—Driven by AI”, Scientific American (from Nature), 10 April 2025, and by AFP, 10 April 2025

Belief in scale is older than today's models. Gordon Moore forecast in 1965 that chips would double in components each year, and Ray Kurzweil later made such curves a law.

Since 2020, scaling laws have put numbers on the belief, and data centres have grown to match. Whether scale alone leads to general intelligence is now the open question.
