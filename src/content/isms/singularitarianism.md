Its roots lie in a remark by John von Neumann, recalled in 1958, and in I. J. Good's 1965 idea of an intelligence explosion. The writer Vernor Vinge named it the singularity in 1983, and Ray Kurzweil gave it dates: 2029 for human-level AI, 2045 for the singularity. Its first institution, the Singularity Institute, founded in 2000 to speed the arrival of smarter-than-human AI, later turned to its risks.

Successionism goes one step further: if machines become our successors, that may be no loss. Samuel Butler raised the prospect in 1863, half in satire, and in 1988 the roboticist Hans Moravec described robots as our mind children, who would outgrow us. Today Richard Sutton calls such succession inevitable, while others, Kurzweil among them, expect humans and machines to merge.
