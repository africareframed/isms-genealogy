AI ethics

Includes algorithmic justice and the ghost-work critique

AI ethics seeks to protect people affected by AI today from biased systems, surveillance, exploited data labour, concentrated corporate power and hype.

Norbert Wiener warned from 1950 that automatic machines could cause mass unemployment. Joseph Weizenbaum held in 1976 that some choices belong to humans alone. From 2014, researchers and journalists showed how scoring systems and face recognition treat groups unequally.

Since 2021 the movement has studied the labour, data and power behind large language models; leading voices call extinction fears a distraction.

Who holds it now

Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR)

Founded by Timnit Gebru on 2 December 2021, a year after she left Google. It aims to counter Big Tech's influence on how AI is researched, built and used.

AI Now Institute

Founded at New York University in 2017 by Kate Crawford and Meredith Whittaker, and now led by Amba Kak and Sarah Myers West. It works against commercial surveillance and power consolidated in very few companies.

Algorithmic Justice League

Joy Buolamwini's organisation, founded in 2016. It combines art and research to show the harms of AI and to give the most affected communities a voice.

Emily M. Bender and Alex Hannasince 2021

A linguist at the University of Washington and DAIR's director of research. They wrote The AI Con (2025) and host Mystery AI Hype Theater 3000, a series that takes apart claims about AI.

ACM FAccT

The yearly conference on fairness, accountability and transparency in socio-technical systems, first held in New York in 2018. Both Gender Shades and Stochastic Parrots appeared there.

Promises

A rights-based democracy where courts, regulators and civil society hold systems to account, weighted toward those most exposed. It echoes civil-rights liberalism and critical theory.

If AI ethics’s demands became law tomorrow, significant systems would be audited before and after launch, and people affected could contest decisions. Deployment would slow while compliance costs rose.

Interests

Older foundations fund most of it. Ford, MacArthur, the Kapor Center, Open Society and Rockefeller gave the Distributed AI Research Institute (DAIR) $3.7 million at its launch in 2021, and MacArthur gave the AI Now Institute $2 million in 2025.

In 2025 ten foundations, among them Ford, MacArthur, Mozilla and Omidyar, committed $500 million to a joint fund, Humanity AI. Data & Society lists Microsoft among its funders in earlier years.

Its demands could serve those who would enforce accountability. Regulators gain, since accountability runs through them, and parties of regulation gain a cause: harms happening now are easier to campaign on than harms to come. Lawyers and advocacy groups gain, since rights need people to enforce them.

Consulting and audit firms stand to gain, since every audit needs an auditor. Firms that use AI in hiring and credit would face audits of their own.

Small firms would lose most, since compliance costs fall hardest on them. Its opponents could gain as well: the largest companies can absorb compliance that smaller rivals cannot, and principles can work as reputation.

2020s

2026Milestone

Cameras on workers' heads

Garment and data workers in India, as reported by Scroll.in, AFP and NPR

Scroll.in traced a video of garment workers with cameras on their heads to a Pearl Global factory, where workers said nobody sought their consent. In Karur, AFP and NPR found textile workers wearing head cameras from Objectways, which sells such data to AI companies. One woman told AFP that filming her housework paid 250 rupees an hour; NPR reported about $2.60 an hour. Many were never told why, NPR reported; Astha Kapoor of the Aapti Institute called it exploitation of labour.

Ayush Tiwari and Raghav Kakkar, “How Big Tech is harnessing the data of Indian factory workers to train robots”, Scroll.in, 20 May 2026 (the video, Pearl Global's Gurugram unit, consent); Aishwarya Kumar, “The Indian workers training AI robots to take their jobs”, AFP, 11 June 2026, as published by The Star (Karur, Objectways, 250 rupees an hour); Diaa Hadid and Omkar Khandekar, “These workers help train robots who could one day steal their jobs”, NPR, 14 October 2026, as published by KPBS (Karur, about $2.60 an hour, Astha Kapoor)

2025Milestone

The AI Con

Emily M. Bender and Alex Hanna

A linguist and a sociologist argue that “AI” is a marketing term, one that “doesn't refer to a coherent set of technologies”. Its hype, they write, lets a few players grow rich on other people's creative work, data and labour. They reject doomers and boosters alike, and see the danger in cheapened labour, data theft and surveillance.

Emily M. Bender and Alex Hanna, The AI Con: How to Fight Big Tech's Hype and Create the Future We Want, Harper (US) and The Bodley Head (UK), May 2025, introduction, sample published by Penguin; review in Publishers Weekly, 15 April 2025

2024Milestone

The TESCREAL bundle

Timnit Gebru and Émile P. Torres

Gebru and Torres argue that the race to build AGI rests on a bundle of ideologies. Their acronym joins transhumanism, extropianism, singularitarianism, cosmism, rationalism, effective altruism and longtermism, which they trace to twentieth-century eugenics. A system as undefined as AGI cannot be tested for safety, they argue, so builders should work on well-defined tasks instead.

Timnit Gebru and Émile P. Torres, “The TESCREAL bundle: Eugenics and the promise of utopia through artificial general intelligence”, First Monday 29(4), April 2024

2023Milestone

Answering the pause letter

Timnit Gebru, Emily M. Bender, Angelina McMillan-Major and Margaret Mitchell

Days after the pause letter, the listed authors of Stochastic Parrots replied. They called its fears of imagined “powerful digital minds” hype, and longtermism a dangerous ideology. The harms, they wrote, are real and present, from exploited workers and data theft to synthetic media and power in few hands. Regulation should demand transparency and hold companies accountable for what their systems produce.

Statement from the listed authors of Stochastic Parrots on the “AI pause” letter, DAIR Institute, 31 March 2023; Devin Coldewey, TechCrunch, 31 March 2023 (names the four authors)

2023Milestone

Less than $2 an hour

Data labellers in Kenya, as reported by Billy Perrigo in TIME

TIME reported that Sama, OpenAI's outsourcing partner in Kenya, paid data labellers about $1.32 to $2 an hour. They labelled passages describing violence, hate speech and sexual abuse, to train a filter meant for ChatGPT. All four workers TIME interviewed said the work left them mentally scarred. Sama disputed the pay figures.

Billy Perrigo, “Exclusive: OpenAI Used Kenyan Workers on Less Than $2 Per Hour to Make ChatGPT Less Toxic”, TIME, 18 January 2023

2021Milestone

On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots

Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and Margaret Mitchell

The four asked whether language models can be too big. They warned of environmental and financial costs, training data too vast to document, and fluent text that seems to mean more than it does. Google asked Gebru to withdraw the paper or remove the names of its employees. Gebru says she was fired in December 2020; Google said it had accepted her resignation.

Emily M. Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major and Shmargaret Shmitchell (Margaret Mitchell), “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?”, FAccT '21, ACM, March 2021, doi:10.1145/3442188.3445922; Karen Hao, “We read the paper that forced Timnit Gebru out of Google. Here's what it says.”, MIT Technology Review, 4 December 2020; Wikipedia, “Timnit Gebru”

2019MilestoneDraft · to be checked

Time off task

Amazon warehouse workers in Baltimore, as reported by Colin Lecher in The Verge

A letter from a lawyer for Amazon to the US labour board described how its warehouses were run. Software tracked each worker's rate and “time off task”, and generated warnings and firings without input from supervisors. About 300 full-time workers at one Baltimore warehouse, roughly a tenth of its staff, were fired over productivity in a year. Amazon denied that anyone is terminated by an automatic system, and said managers can step in.

Colin Lecher, “How Amazon automatically tracks and fires warehouse workers for ‘productivity’”, The Verge, 25 April 2019, as reported by CBS News, “Amazon under fire for software that recommends firing workers”, April 2019 (the letter of 4 September 2018 to the National Labor Relations Board; about 300 fired at the Baltimore site between August 2017 and September 2018, roughly 10% of its workforce; Amazon's reply that managers can intervene); KUOW, “Amazon denies report that it fires workers by robot”, 26 April 2019 (the denial)

2018Milestone

Gender Shades

Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru

Buolamwini, then a graduate student at MIT, and Gebru tested three commercial systems that classify gender from faces, from IBM, Microsoft and Face++. Darker-skinned women were misclassified up to 34.7% of the time, lighter-skinned men at most 0.8%. The study appeared at the first conference on fairness, accountability and transparency, in New York.

Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru, “Gender Shades: Intersectional Accuracy Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification”, Proceedings of the 1st Conference on Fairness, Accountability and Transparency, PMLR 81, 2018, pp. 77–91; Wikipedia, “Joy Buolamwini” (the three companies)

2017Institutional root

The AI Now Institute

Kate Crawford and Meredith Whittaker

Crawford and Whittaker launched the AI Now Institute at New York University in November 2017. It set out to study the social implications of AI, from civil rights and biased data to labour and automation. Today, led by Amba Kak and Sarah Myers West, it opposes commercial surveillance and power consolidated in very few companies.

NYU Tandon School of Engineering, “New Artificial Intelligence Research Institute Launches”, 20 November 2017; AI Now Institute, “About”, ainowinstitute.org

2016Milestone

Weapons of Math Destruction

Cathy O'Neil

The mathematician Cathy O'Neil, who had left finance, named the harmful algorithms of her book's title. Models now decide on school places, jobs, loans and health insurance, she argued, yet they are unregulated and hard to contest. They reinforce discrimination, rewarding the advantaged and punishing the poor.

Cathy O'Neil, Weapons of Math Destruction: How Big Data Increases Inequality and Threatens Democracy, Crown, September 2016

2016MilestoneDraft · to be checked

State v. Loomis

Eric Loomis and the Wisconsin Supreme Court

A judge had cited Eric Loomis's high COMPAS scores in sentencing him to six years in prison. He argued that a score from closed-source software he could not examine violated his right to due process. The Wisconsin Supreme Court disagreed, but required warnings that the tool is proprietary and that studies have questioned its fairness by race. In June 2017 the US Supreme Court declined to hear the case.

Lauren Kirchner, “Wisconsin Court: Warning Labels Are Needed for Scores Rating Defendants' Risk of Future Crime”, ProPublica, 14 July 2016 (the ruling and its four warnings); State v. Loomis, 2016 WI 68, 881 N.W.2d 749 (Wis. 2016), filed 13 July 2016; “State v. Loomis”, Harvard Law Review, vol. 130, p. 1530, March 2017; Wikipedia, “Loomis v. Wisconsin” (certiorari denied on 26 June 2017)

2016Milestone

Machine Bias

Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, ProPublica

ProPublica examined COMPAS, software that scores how likely defendants are to reoffend, using records of over 7,000 people in Broward County, Florida. Black defendants who did not reoffend were almost twice as likely as white ones to have been labelled higher risk. Northpointe, the maker, disputed the analysis.

Julia Angwin, Jeff Larson, Surya Mattu and Lauren Kirchner, “Machine Bias”, ProPublica, 23 May 2016

2014Institutional root

Fairness, accountability, transparency

Solon Barocas and Moritz Hardt

Barocas and Hardt organised a one-day workshop in Montreal, at the NIPS machine-learning conference, on fairness, accountability and transparency in machine learning. It addressed growing anxiety about machine learning in decisions on jobs, health care, education and policing. Workshops followed each year to 2018, when a first conference under the same three words met in New York. The field's textbook dates a new research community from this workshop.

“Fairness, Accountability, and Transparency in Machine Learning”, workshop at NIPS 2014, organised by Moritz Hardt and Solon Barocas (nips.cc; fatml.org, schedules 2014–2018); Cathy O'Neil, “Fairness, accountability, and transparency in big data models”, mathbabe.org, 16 December 2014 (one day, Montreal); Solon Barocas, Moritz Hardt and Arvind Narayanan, Fairness and Machine Learning, fairmlbook.org, introduction, bibliographic notes; FAT* 2018, New York (facctconference.org)

1990s

1996Claimed ancestor · cited

Bias in Computer Systems

Batya Friedman and Helen Nissenbaum

Friedman and Nissenbaum called a system biased if it discriminates against some people, systematically and unfairly, in favour of others. From actual cases they drew three kinds, preexisting bias from society, technical bias from design and emergent bias from use. Freedom from bias, they argued, should rank with reliability and accuracy. The field's textbook, Fairness and Machine Learning, cites it as an early work on algorithmic fairness.

Batya Friedman and Helen Nissenbaum, “Bias in Computer Systems”, ACM Transactions on Information Systems 14(3), 1996, pp. 330–347; Solon Barocas, Moritz Hardt and Arvind Narayanan, Fairness and Machine Learning, fairmlbook.org, introduction, bibliographic notes

1970s

1976Claimed ancestor · cited

Computer Power and Human Reason

Joseph Weizenbaum

Weizenbaum, who wrote the ELIZA chatbot at MIT, was shocked that many users took it seriously and opened their hearts to it. In this book he argued that whether computers can match people is a separate question from whether they should be given a task. Deciding can be programmed, he wrote, but choosing takes human judgement. Emily M. Bender and Alex Hanna cite his alarm as an early warning against AI hype.

Joseph Weizenbaum, Computer Power and Human Reason: From Judgment to Calculation, W. H. Freeman, 1976; Wikipedia, “Joseph Weizenbaum”; Emily M. Bender and Alex Hanna, The AI Con, Harper, 2025, excerpted as “On the Very Real Dangers of the Artificial Intelligence Hype Machine”, Literary Hub, 13 May 2025

1950s

1950Attributed by historians

The Human Use of Human Beings

Norbert Wiener

Two years after Cybernetics, Wiener explored the ethical questions computers would raise, unemployment among them. In the revised edition of 1954 he likened automatic machines to slave labour and foresaw mass unemployment. He urged that they be used for human benefit, not merely for profit. The philosopher Terrell Bynum calls him the founder of computer ethics, though Wiener did not coin the term.

Norbert Wiener, The Human Use of Human Beings: Cybernetics and Society, Houghton Mifflin, 1950; second edition, 1954, chapter IX; Terrell Bynum, “Computer and Information Ethics”, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2001, revised 2015
