Reclaim · Introduction

Neo-Luddism

Includes the analogue revival and Marxist neo-Luddism

Neo-Luddism holds that people should take back from Big Tech the power to choose which machines enter their lives, and to refuse AI.

Its writers want workers and citizens, not tech companies, to decide which machines to accept, AI included. The Luddites, they argue, fought not machines but their use against workers. Today they oppose AI that cuts jobs, takes artists’ work and harvests attention, and many gather offline to build alternatives to Big Tech.

The name comes from English textile workers who broke machines from 1811 in the name of Ned Ludd. In 1990 the psychologist Chellis Glendinning published a neo-Luddite manifesto: all technologies are political, she wrote, and neo-Luddites are not anti-technology. In his account of the neo-Luddites, Kirkpatrick Sale put Lewis Mumford first among the critics behind them, with Jacques Ellul and Ivan Illich.

The rise of AI revived the name. In 2023 Brian Merchant’s Blood in the Machine tied the Luddites to the Hollywood writers’ strike and the fight over automation. Student Luddite clubs, the podcast This Machine Kills and New York’s Summer of Ludd now wear the name with pride.

Who holds it now

Brian Merchant

Tech journalist who calls himself a Luddite. He wrote Blood in the Machine (2023), and his newsletter of the same name follows what he calls the Luddite renaissance.

This Machine Kills

Weekly podcast on technology and political economy, running since 2020. Its hosts are Jathan Sadowski, a proud Luddite, and Edward Ongweso Jr., billed as a born-again Luddite.

The Luddite Club

Club begun by a Brooklyn high-school student and named after Ned Ludd; its most devoted members carry flip phones. By 2025 it was a non-profit with more than 20 chapters at US high schools and colleges.

Luddite Renaissance

Anonymous New York collective behind the Summer of Ludd in 2026, a week of free events for a life less reliant on Big Tech. One was a mock trial of OpenAI.

Gavin Mueller

Media scholar at the University of Amsterdam. His Breaking Things at Work (2021) makes the case for a Marxist neo-Luddism that resists technologies used to discipline workers.

Courtney C. Radsch

Nonresident fellow at the Brookings Institution. In 2025 she argued that we should all be Luddites about AI, putting labour, agency and justice before speed and scale.

Promises

Technology decided where it lands, in the workplace, the town and the classroom. It echoes the Luddites themselves, William Morris and the labour movement.

If Neo-Luddism’s demands became law tomorrow, workers and communities would gain vetoes. AI would enter a workplace only by agreement, and adoption would become uneven, place by place.

Interests

No major funders found. Its leverage is organised labour instead. In 2023 a 148-day strike won the Writers Guild of America a contract under which writers decide whether and how generative AI is used.

Its demands could serve those who already hold power at work. Strong unions and established professions, teachers and academics among them, stand to gain, since a veto is only as strong as the group holding it. Brands that sell the handmade gain too, as refusal turns the human-made into a premium.

Populists on the left and the right could draw on its anger at automation. Local councils would gain a say, while national industrial strategies would lose speed. Big Tech would lose: adoption would need consent, place by place.

Its opponents could gain as well. Refusal in one place moves the work to competitors in places that don’t refuse.

2020s

2026Milestone

The Summer of Ludd

Luddite Renaissance, an anonymous New York collective

Around the Fourth of July an anonymous collective held a week of free New York events, for a life less reliant on Big Tech. At a mock trial in Washington Square Park the crowd found OpenAI and Sam Altman guilty, then stomped on a cardboard ChatGPT phone. Talks covered data centres, surveillance and AI, and word spread by posters and paper guides, not social media.

Janus Rose, “Being a Luddite Is Fun Again”, 404 Media, 27 July 2026 (week-long festival of free events over the July 4th weekend; mock trial; posters and paper event guides); Vitasta Kaul, “Return of the Luddites”, ThePrint, 4 July 2026 (discussions on data centres, surveillance and AI; posters and printed pamphlets); Emma Bowman, “Get with the times — here's what a ‘Luddite’ means today”, NPR, 19 June 2026 (social-media free events)

2025Milestone

The Luddite renaissance

Brian Merchant; youth collectives and workers' groups

Brian Merchant declared a Luddite renaissance in full swing, listing youth collectives, workers' groups and events against Big Tech and AI. In New York several dozen people gathered for a Luddite Renaissance rally. Outside an Apple store in Manhattan, a figure dressed as Death smashed an iPhone with a rock. Like the first Luddites, CNN reported, the new ones oppose exploitation, not technology.

Brian Merchant, “The Luddite Renaissance is in full swing”, Blood in the Machine, 21 September 2025; Allison Morrow, “As AI gets more life-like, a new Luddite movement is taking root”, CNN, 8 October 2025, via AOL and CNN Brasil (the rally “last month”; not anti-technology but anti-exploitation); Mark Hurst, “The Luddite renaissance is here”, Creative Good, 23 October 2025, buttondown.com/creativegood (the Apple Store on West 14th Street, about two weeks earlier)

2023Milestone

Blood in the Machine

Brian Merchant

A tech journalist tells the story of “the first machines that came for human jobs”. The Luddites, he argues, wanted a part in the new technology, not to be replaced by it. He calls himself a Luddite, and holds that their history explains the power and toll of Big Tech and AI today.

Brian Merchant, Blood in the Machine: The Origins of the Rebellion Against Big Tech, Little, Brown and Company, 26 September 2023 (publisher's description, hachettebookgroup.com); KQED Forum, “Why Brian Merchant, a Tech Columnist, Says He's a Luddite”, 14 November 2023

2023Milestone

The writers' strike and AI

Writers Guild of America; Brian Merchant

After a strike fought in part over studios' plans for AI, Hollywood writers won a new contract. It says AI is not a writer, and studios cannot require writers to use it. Brian Merchant called the strike the first workplace battle between humans and AI. He said the outcome was one the Luddites would applaud.

Writers Guild of America, “Know Your Rights: Artificial Intelligence” (the 2023 MBA's AI provisions), wga.org; James Robins, “Can a New Generation of Luddites Take Down A.I.?”, The New Republic, 6 November 2023 (the strike in part against AI); Brian Merchant, “The writers' strike was the first workplace battle between humans and AI. The humans won”, Los Angeles Times, September 2023 (listed by the Benton Institute, Daily Digest 25 September 2023); Libby Rainey, “‘Blood In The Machine’ Connects Hollywood Workers Fight Against AI To The Luddites”, LAist, 2 October 2023

2022Milestone

The Luddite Club

High-school students in Brooklyn

The New York Times reported on a club of Brooklyn teenagers who meet in Prospect Park on Sundays, phones put away. The most committed carry flip phones, and they read, draw and paint instead of scrolling. They named the club after Ned Ludd. By 2025 it was a non-profit with more than 20 school and college chapters in the US.

Alex Vadukul, “‘Luddite’ Teens Don't Want Your Likes”, The New York Times, 15 December 2022, republished as “‘Social media and phones are not real life’: Meet the teens ditching likes for flip phones”, The Irish Times, 26 December 2022; Allison Morrow, “As AI gets more life-like, a new Luddite movement is taking root”, CNN, 8 October 2025

2021Milestone

I'm a Luddite. You should be one, too

Jathan Sadowski

A social scientist at Monash University calls himself a proud Luddite. He sees in the backlash against Big Tech, gig platforms and data extraction the seeds of a neo-Luddite movement. Technology, he argues, should be democratically governed, not treated as a grab-bag of apps and gadgets. Since 2020 he has co-hosted the podcast This Machine Kills with Edward Ongweso Jr.

Jathan Sadowski, “I'm a Luddite. You should be one, too”, The Conversation, republished by Monash Lens, 18 August 2021; Apple Podcasts, This Machine Kills (2020–2026, hosted by Jathan Sadowski and Edward Ongweso Jr.); Edward Ongweso Jr., edwardongweso.com (a podcast “we started in 2020”)

2021Milestone

Breaking Things at Work

Gavin Mueller

A media scholar argues that the Luddites were right about why people hate their jobs. He traces resistance from textile mills to algorithms, and calls a Marxist neo-Luddism crucial to changing the world. Against left-wing hopes that automation will free workers, he holds that their power depends on subverting such technologies.

Gavin Mueller, Breaking Things at Work: The Luddites Are Right About Why You Hate Your Job, Verso, February 2021 (publisher's description, versobooks.com); New Books Network, “Breaking Things at Work” (book description); James Muldoon, “Fully Decelerated Carbon-Neutral Luddism”, Verso blog, 26 April 2021

1990s

1996Milestone

The Second Luddite Congress

Center for Plain Living; Kirkpatrick Sale

About 250 delegates met at a Quaker meeting house in Barnesville, Ohio, convened by the Center for Plain Living, a conservative Quaker group. Kirkpatrick Sale gave the keynote, and speakers included Bill McKibben and Clifford Stoll. It opened days after the Unabomber's arrest; delegates voiced little sympathy for him and rejected the first Luddites' violence. A draft statement held “that the needs of people exceed the needs of the machines”.

Lionel Basney, “Questioning Progress”, Books & Culture, September/October 1998; Joshua Glenn, “Beardless in Barnesville”, Utne Reader, July–August 1996, reposted on HiLobrow, 29 November 2011 (the draft Statement of Means); The editors of E Magazine, “Second Luddite Congress in Ohio: A Modern Critique of Technology”, emagazine.com, posted 20 July 2004 (“little sympathy was expressed for the Unabomber”; the original Luddites' violence rejected)

1995Milestone

Rebels Against the Future

Kirkpatrick Sale

Sale's history of the Luddites gives its last third to the harms of today's technology, among them “disposable jobs”. In January he smashed a computer with a sledgehammer on stage at an Utne Reader forum. In Wired he bet Kevin Kelly $1,000 that currency collapse, war between rich and poor and environmental disaster would converge by 2020. The referee ruled for Kelly, saying it was a squeaker.

Kirkpatrick Sale, Rebels Against the Future: The Luddites and Their War on the Industrial Revolution, Addison-Wesley, 1995, reviewed by Kirkus Reviews (release date 1 May 1995; the last third on current technology); Monika Bauerlein, “The Luddites are Back”, Utne Reader, March–April 1996 (the sledgehammer, January 1995; the Wired debate, June 1995); Kevin Kelly, “Concluding our 25-year Bet”, The Technium, 5 January 2021

1992Attributed by historians

Technopoly

Neil Postman

A media critic argues that culture has surrendered to technology, which now shapes what politics, education and truth mean. He is sometimes listed among the neo-Luddites. A year later he called himself “not at all a Luddite”, though he saw no disgrace in being linked with them.

Neil Postman, Technopoly: The Surrender of Culture to Technology, Knopf, 1992 (Open Library, Vintage edition of 1993, “originally published” by Knopf in 1992; Penguin Random House, publisher's description); Neil Postman, “Of Luddites, Learning, and Life”, TECHNOS Quarterly, vol. 2 no. 4, Winter 1993; Wikipedia, “Neo-Luddism” (lists him as a prominent neo-Luddite who did not consider himself one)

1990Modern definition

Notes toward a Neo-Luddite Manifesto

Chellis Glendinning

A New Mexico psychologist recasts the Luddites as a thoughtful social movement, and their heirs as citizens who doubt technology means progress. “Neo-Luddites are not anti-technology,” she writes; every technology is political and serves particular interests. Her programme calls for dismantling destructive technologies, computers among them, and for tools made at human scale by those who use them.

Chellis Glendinning, “Notes toward a Neo-Luddite Manifesto”, Utne Reader, 1990 (Wikipedia, “Neo-Luddism”, references), text at The Anarchist Library; Kirkpatrick Sale, “The Neo-Luddites”, in the zine Neo-Luddites and Lessons from the Luddites, Crow's Nest Distribution, at The Anarchist Library (“a New Mexico psychologist”, March 1990)

1980s

1987Claimed ancestor · cited

Why I Am Not Going to Buy a Computer

Wendell Berry

A Kentucky farmer and writer explains why he writes by hand, partly because a computer would tie his work to strip-mined coal. He sets standards for any new tool, among them that it be cheaper, smaller, repairable near home and disruptive of nothing good. When called a Luddite, he came to the Luddites' defence. A critic later called his writing the unconfessed text of the 1996 Luddite congress.

Wendell Berry, “Why I Am Not Going to Buy a Computer”, Harper's, 1987 (study notes, College of Charleston); Mark Engler, “Remembering Wendell Berry, agrarian writer and voice of conscience”, Resilience, 2 September 2026 (when branded a Luddite, he rises to their defence); Lionel Basney, “Questioning Progress”, Books & Culture, September/October 1998

1984Claimed ancestor · cited

Is It O.K. to Be a Luddite?

Thomas Pynchon

A novelist traces the Luddites to the legend of Ned Lud, said to have broken two knitting frames in 1779. He reads their machine-breaking as class war against the capital each machine stood for and the work it took away. The next great challenge, he predicts, will come when artificial intelligence, molecular biology and robotics converge. Kirkpatrick Sale later wrote that Pynchon had called himself a Luddite.

Thomas Pynchon, “Is It O.K. to Be a Luddite?”, The New York Times Book Review, 28 October 1984, pp. 1, 40–41 (text posted to the nettime list, 8 January 1996); Kirkpatrick Sale, “The Neo-Luddites”, Crow's Nest Distribution zine, at The Anarchist Library (Pynchon “has said it”)

1970s

1973Claimed ancestor · cited

Tools for Conviviality

Ivan Illich

An Austrian priest and social critic sets convivial tools, which widen what people can freely do, against industrial tools that end up destroying autonomy. Past a second watershed, he argues, a tool's benefits are used to justify manipulation by a professional elite. Kirkpatrick Sale names Illich among the critics who challenged the technocratic mainstream before the neo-Luddites.

Ivan Illich, Tools for Conviviality, Harper & Row, 1973 (Wikipedia, “Tools for Conviviality”); Kirkpatrick Sale, “The Neo-Luddites”, Crow's Nest Distribution zine, at The Anarchist Library

1950s

1954Claimed ancestor · cited

The Technological Society

Jacques Ellul

A French sociologist and lay theologian describes technique, the totality of rationally reached methods, as wider than machines and ruled by efficiency. “The only thing that matters technically is yield, production,” he writes. Published in French in 1954, it appeared in English in 1964. Kirkpatrick Sale names Ellul among the critics who challenged the technocratic mainstream.

Jacques Ellul, La Technique ou l'Enjeu du siècle, 1954; The Technological Society, translated by John Wilkinson, 1964 (Wikipedia, “The Technological Society”; Wikipedia, “Neo-Luddism”, for the quotation, citing The Technological Society, p. 324); Kirkpatrick Sale, “The Neo-Luddites”, Crow's Nest Distribution zine, at The Anarchist Library

c. 1910Claimed ancestor · citedDraft · to be checked

The telephone stays outside

Old Order Amish of Lancaster County, Pennsylvania

Around 1910 Amish bishops in Lancaster County, Pennsylvania, decided their people should not have telephones, which belonged to “the world” and spread gossip. The dispute helped split the church, which lost a fifth of its members. Today phones stand in shared booths away from the house, and each Amish group draws its own lines on which machines come in. Wendell Berry held up their horse-worked farms as models; in 2026 the Amish of North America numbered about 421,000, doubling about every twenty years.

Diane Zimmerman Umble, “Shunning the Telephone”, Invention & Technology, vol. 5 no. 1, Spring/Summer 1989 (the 1910 division over the telephone; a fifth of members lost; the phone of “the world”, enabling gossip; community telephones, shared and for emergencies); Will Braun, “Seven criteria for the adoption of new technology”, Geez, 18 November 2010 (phone shanties allowed, phones in homes not; restrictions vary among some 40 Amish groups); Bill McKibben, “Prophet in Kentucky”, The New York Review of Books, 14 June 1990 (Berry admires Amish farms and communities as useful models); Young Center for Anabaptist and Pietist Studies, “Amish Population Profile, 2026”, Elizabethtown College (421,200 as of June 2026; doubling about every 20 years)

1880s

1884Claimed ancestor · citedDraft · to be checked

Useful Work versus Useless Toil

William Morris

A designer and socialist tells a London club that “labour-saving” machines have not saved labour: they turn skilled workers into unskilled ones. In a true society, he argues, the same machines would cut the hours of unattractive work, and work itself would give pleasure. He had learned this from John Ruskin's “The Nature of Gothic” (1853), which he later printed at his Kelmscott Press. In Breaking Things at Work (2021), Gavin Mueller enlists Morris against the promise that machines bring abundance.

William Morris, “Useful Work versus Useless Toil”, lecture to the Hampstead Liberal Club, 1884 (booklet note, worrydream.com), text at the Marxists Internet Archive (“labour-saving” machines; machines in a true society; the hope of pleasure in work); William Morris, preface to John Ruskin, The Nature of Gothic, Kelmscott Press, 1892, at the Marxists Internet Archive (“man''s pleasure in labour”); John Ruskin, The Nature of Gothic, printed by William Morris at the Kelmscott Press, 1892, Internet Archive; Wikipedia, “The Stones of Venice (book)” (the chapter in volume 2, 1853); Zachary Loeb, “Specters of Ludd”, review of Gavin Mueller, Breaking Things at Work (Verso, 2021), boundary 2 (Morris against the promise of abundance from machines)

The Luddites

English textile workers

From 1811 weavers and knitters in Nottinghamshire, Yorkshire and Lancashire broke machines in the name of a mythical leader, General Ned Ludd. They protested against wage cuts, lost work and a market that no longer paid a set price for craft. Neo-Luddites take their name from them, and Glendinning's manifesto recast them as a thoughtful movement defending livelihoods and communities.

The National Archives, “Why did the Luddites protest?”, education resource; Chellis Glendinning, “Notes toward a Neo-Luddite Manifesto”, Utne Reader, March 1990
