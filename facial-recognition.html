Vocabulary · Introduction

Facial recognition

Facial recognition software identifies people from their faces, and police use of it has led to bans from San Francisco to the EU.

Facial recognition compares a face in a photo or video with faces held in a database. Police use live cameras to look for wanted people in crowds; in one year London’s police scanned more than three million faces. The EU’s law speaks more widely of remote biometric identification.

Automated facial recognition began in 1964–66, when Woody Bledsoe, Helen Chan and Charles Bisson of Panoramic Research in Palo Alto built a “man-machine” system. An operator marked facial features on a RAND tablet, and the computer compared distances between them. An unnamed intelligence agency funded the work.

It entered the AI debate through studies of bias and the spread of police use. Researchers found higher error rates for some groups, and in 2019 San Francisco banned its police from using it. In 2025 the EU’s first AI bans began to apply.

AI ethics

For AI ethics, facial recognition is a central case of bias. In Gender Shades (2018) Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru found commercial systems misread darker-skinned women far more often than lighter-skinned men.

Strands

Gender Shades

In 2018 Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru tested commercial systems that classify gender from faces. Darker-skinned women were misclassified at rates of up to 34.7%, lighter-skinned men at no more than 0.8%. In 2019 a US government study of 189 face recognition algorithms found demographic differences in most, though results varied widely between them.

Joy Buolamwini and Timnit Gebru, “Gender Shades: Intersectional Accuracy Disparities in Commercial Gender Classification”, Proceedings of the 1st Conference on Fairness, Accountability and Transparency, PMLR 81, 2018; National Institute of Standards and Technology, “NIST Study Evaluates Effects of Race, Age, Sex on Face Recognition Software”, 19 December 2019

Clearview AI

Clearview AI built a searchable database of some 30 billion images, scraped from the internet without people's consent. In September 2024 the Dutch data protection authority fined it €30.5 million under the GDPR. The EU's AI Act now bans such untargeted scraping of facial images.

Natasha Lomas, “Clearview AI hit with its largest GDPR fine yet as Dutch regulator considers holding execs personally liable”, TechCrunch, 3 September 2024; Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act), Article 5(1)(e)

The bans

In May 2019 San Francisco became the first US city to ban its police from using facial recognition. Since 2 February 2025 the EU's AI Act has banned real-time remote biometric identification in public spaces for policing. Exceptions include searches for missing persons, imminent threats to life and suspects of serious crimes.

Associated Press, “San Francisco bans use of facial recognition technology by police”, PBS NewsHour, 14 May 2019; Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (Artificial Intelligence Act), Article 5(1)(h); “Implementation Timeline”, artificialintelligenceact.eu

Who disputes it

London's Metropolitan Police defends it, and its lead officer calls it game-changing. Live facial recognition led to 962 arrests between September 2024 and September 2025, the force reports, with 10 false alerts. Eight of those ten involved Black people, and Big Brother Watch calls it “an Orwellian and authoritarian technology” (The Register, 2025).
