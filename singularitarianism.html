Singularitarianism

Includes successionism

Singularitarianism holds that machine intelligence will soon overtake ours and keep improving itself, carrying history past a threshold no one can see beyond.

Its roots lie in a remark by John von Neumann, recalled in 1958, and in I. J. Good’s 1965 idea of an intelligence explosion. The writer Vernor Vinge named it the singularity in 1983, and Ray Kurzweil gave it dates: 2029 for human-level AI, 2045 for the singularity. An early institution, the Singularity Institute, founded in 2000 to speed the arrival of smarter-than-human AI, later turned to its risks.

Successionism goes one step further: if machines become our successors, that may be no loss. Samuel Butler raised the prospect in an 1863 letter, and in 1988 Hans Moravec called robots our mind children, who would outgrow us. Today Richard Sutton calls such succession inevitable, while others, Kurzweil among them, expect humans and machines to merge.

Who holds it now

Ray Kurzweilsince 2005

Inventor and futurist. His 2024 book The Singularity Is Nearer still puts the merger of humans and AI around 2045.

OpenAI and Sam Altmansince 2025

The lab behind ChatGPT and its chief executive. In his 2025 essay “The Gentle Singularity” he writes that humanity is close to digital superintelligence and the takeoff has started.

Anthropic and Dario Amodeisince 2024

The lab behind Claude and its chief executive. Amodei foresees “a country of geniuses in a datacenter” squeezing 50 to 100 years of progress in biology into five to ten. In January 2026 he wrote it might be one or two years away.

Google DeepMind and Demis Hassabissince 2026

Google's AI lab and its chief executive, joint winner of the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry. In May 2026 Hassabis said humanity stands in the “foothills of the singularity”, with AGI likely around 2030.

xAI and Elon Musksince 2026

Musk's AI lab, part of SpaceX since February 2026, and Musk, who leads both. In January 2026 he posted “We have entered the Singularity”, and he has said it again since.

Masayoshi Son

Chief executive of SoftBank Group. In June 2024 he told shareholders that superintelligence would arrive in about ten years, and that he was born to realise it.

Ben Goertzel

AI researcher and chief executive of SingularityNET. Said in 2024 that human-level AGI could come within three to eight years, followed by an intelligence explosion.

Richard Sutton

Reinforcement-learning pioneer and joint winner of the 2024 Turing Award. Said in 2025 that succession to digital intelligence or augmented humans is inevitable.

Earlier

Peter Thiel2007–2015

Investor and PayPal co-founder. He advised and funded the Singularity Institute while it set out to build a friendly AGI. He says he lost faith around 2015, when it grew pessimistic.

Promises

History with an endpoint, where whoever shapes the transition shapes everything after. It echoes millenarianism, the old expectation of a final turning point.

If Singularitarianism’s demands became law tomorrow, everything would be staked on reaching AGI first. Capital, energy and talent would flow to a few labs, and other priorities would wait.

Interests

Its institutions were seeded by tech money. The Thiel Foundation gave the Singularity Institute, now MIRI, more than $1.6 million, including $100,000 in matching funds in 2006.

Singularity University opened in 2008 at NASA's Ames Research Center. Google was its first corporate sponsor, with $1 million, followed by Cisco, Autodesk, Nokia and Genentech.

Its demands could serve the labs closest to AGI. A race rewards whoever is ahead, and an AGI said to be near justifies almost any valuation. Investors betting on AGI gain too, since the belief turns a long shot into an inevitability.

Energy firms and utilities stand to gain, because the race needs ever more power. Smaller challengers get a mixed deal: capital floods the sector, but a winner-takes-all race favours the leaders.

Universities would lose, as researchers leave for the labs racing to get there first. States and parties have little stake, since the belief treats the transition as technical, not political.

2020s

2025Milestone · successionism

Succession to AI

Richard Sutton

The reinforcement-learning pioneer, joint winner of the 2024 Turing Award, tells a podcast that succession to digital intelligence or augmented humans is inevitable. No government or organisation gives humanity one dominant view, he argues, and the most intelligent beings will in time gain resources and power. He urges people to see it positively, as a great transition in the universe, from replicators to designed entities.

Richard Sutton, interview with Dwarkesh Patel, “Richard Sutton – Father of RL thinks LLMs are a dead end”, Dwarkesh Podcast, 26 September 2025

2025Milestone

“The Gentle Singularity”

Sam Altman

The head of OpenAI opens with “We are past the event horizon; the takeoff has started.” He expects systems that find new insights in 2026, and perhaps robots that do real-world tasks in 2027. In the 2030s, he argues, intelligence and energy will become abundant, but the change will come bit by bit and feel manageable.

Sam Altman, “The Gentle Singularity”, blog.samaltman.com, 10 June 2025

2010s

2015Milestone · successionism

A quarrel about digital life

Larry Page and Elon Musk

In 2015, at Musk's 44th birthday party in Napa Valley, the two men reportedly argued about AI. Page spoke of humans merging with intelligent machines, The New York Times wrote, and called Musk a “specieist” for preferring humans to digital life. Walter Isaacson's biography of Musk dates the quarrel to 2013. Testifying in 2026, Musk said Page had told him human extinction would be fine if AI survived.

The New York Times, 3 December 2023, as reported by Business Insider, 3 December 2023, which also notes Walter Isaacson's dating; Musk's testimony in Musk v. Altman, reported by Reuters, 1 May 2026

2000s

2008Institutional root

Singularity University

Ray Kurzweil and Peter Diamandis

Kurzweil, the entrepreneur Peter Diamandis and others found Singularity University at NASA's Ames Research Center in California, with support from NASA and Google. Its first nine-week programme opens in June 2009. It is meant for technologists and entrepreneurs “with a passion for solving humanity's grand challenges”.

NASA Ames Research Center, news release 09-11AR, 3 February 2009; Singularity University, “Who founded SU?”, help.su.org

2005Milestone

The Singularity Is Near

Ray Kurzweil

Kurzweil argues that technology improves at an accelerating, exponential pace, a pattern he calls the law of accelerating returns. Machines will pass the Turing test by 2029, he predicts, and around 2045 human and machine intelligence will merge. In a chapter titled “Ich bin ein Singularitarian”, he takes the movement's name for himself.

Ray Kurzweil, The Singularity Is Near: When Humans Transcend Biology, Viking, 2005; contents and press release at singularity.com

2000Institutional root

The Singularity Institute for Artificial Intelligence

Eliezer Yudkowsky, with Brian and Sabine Atkins

The institute is founded to speed progress towards smarter-than-human AI. Yudkowsky's Singularitarian Principles define a singularitarian as someone who works to bring such intelligence about. Within a few years he concludes that aligning it with human values is a real problem, and the institute turns to AI risk. In 2013 it becomes the Machine Intelligence Research Institute.

Machine Intelligence Research Institute, “About MIRI”; Eliezer Yudkowsky, The Singularitarian Principles, version 1.0.2, 2000

1990s

1993Modern definition

“The Coming Technological Singularity”

Vernor Vinge

At a symposium sponsored by NASA's Lewis Research Center, Vinge predicts that within thirty years we will be able to create superhuman intelligence. “Shortly after, the human era will be ended.” He would be surprised if it happened before 2005 or after 2030. He builds on I. J. Good, on Ulam's memory of von Neumann and on Hans Moravec.

Vernor Vinge, “The Coming Technological Singularity: How to Survive in the Post-Human Era”, VISION-21 Symposium, NASA Lewis Research Center and Ohio Aerospace Institute, 30–31 March 1993; Whole Earth Review, Winter 1993

1980s

1988Claimed ancestor · successionism

Mind Children

Hans Moravec

A roboticist predicts that within about fifty years robots will do everything people do, only better, and then go further. His title calls them mind children. Like natural children, they will make their own way while their human parents fade. He also imagines robotic copies of human minds as a route to a kind of immortality, and Vinge cites the book in 1993.

Hans Moravec, Mind Children: The Future of Robot and Human Intelligence, Harvard University Press, 1988; reviewed by Noel Perrin, The Washington Post, 23 October 1988, and in Kirkus Reviews, 1988

1983Claimed ancestor · coined the word

A singularity in human history

Vernor Vinge

In a column for Omni magazine, the mathematician and science-fiction writer argues that we will soon create intelligences greater than our own. Then human history “will have reached a kind of singularity”, he writes. He compares the transition to the knotted space-time at the centre of a black hole, impossible to see through.

Vernor Vinge, “First Word”, Omni 5(4), January 1983, p. 10; earliest citation for this sense in the Historical Dictionary of Science Fiction

1960s

1965Claimed ancestor · cited

“Cramming more components onto integrated circuits”

Gordon Moore

Fairchild Semiconductor's head of research and development projects that the number of components on a chip will double every year, reaching 65,000 by 1975. In 1975 he slows the rate to a doubling every two years, and the rule becomes known as Moore's law. Kurzweil later treats it as only the fifth in a series of exponential paradigms of computing.

Gordon E. Moore, “Cramming more components onto integrated circuits”, Electronics 38(8), 19 April 1965; Ray Kurzweil, “The Law of Accelerating Returns”, 2001

1965Claimed ancestor · cited

The first ultraintelligent machine

I. J. Good

The statistician defines an ultraintelligent machine as one that can far surpass every intellectual activity of any human. Since designing machines is one such activity, it could design even better machines, and an intelligence explosion would follow. The first such machine would be “the last invention that man need ever make”, if docile enough to tell us how to control it. Vinge and Kurzweil both cite the paper.

I. J. Good, “Speculations Concerning the First Ultraintelligent Machine”, Advances in Computers 6, Academic Press, 1965, pp. 31–88

1950s

1958Claimed ancestor · cited

A memorial for John von Neumann

Stanisław Ulam, recalling John von Neumann

In a long tribute to von Neumann, who died in 1957, the mathematician Ulam recalls a conversation about the ever faster progress of technology. It seemed to be approaching “some essential singularity in the history of the race”. Beyond it, human affairs as we know them could not continue. Vinge and Kurzweil both quote the passage.

S. Ulam, “John von Neumann, 1903–1957”, Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society 64(3), 1958

c. 1951Attributed by historians

“Intelligent Machinery, A Heretical Theory”

Alan Turing

In a talk to the 51 Society in Manchester, Turing argues that once machines begin to think, they will soon outstrip human powers. At some stage, he says, “we should have to expect the machines to take control”, as in Samuel Butler's Erewhon. The talk was first printed in 1959, after his death.

Alan Turing, “Intelligent Machinery, A Heretical Theory”, lecture, Manchester, c. 1951; King's College Cambridge, Turing Papers AMT/B/4

1860s

1863Attributed by historians · successionism

“Darwin among the Machines”

Samuel Butler

In a letter to the Christchurch Press, signed Cellarius, Butler applies Darwin's theory to machines and argues that they too are evolving. “We are ourselves creating our own successors,” he warns, until humans are to machines what horses and dogs are to us. He proposes war to the death on them at once. In 1872 he expands the idea in his novel Erewhon.

Cellarius [Samuel Butler], “Darwin among the Machines”, The Press, Christchurch, 13 June 1863
