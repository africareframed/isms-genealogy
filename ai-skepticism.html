Doubt · Introduction

AI skepticism

Includes the hype critique, the neurosymbolic critique and the bubble thesis

AI skepticism holds that today’s AI imitates understanding without having it, and that the promise of AGI is hype to be tested.

Its writers hold that large language models predict words without understanding them: in 2021 Emily M. Bender and her co-authors called them stochastic parrots. Gary Marcus argues that scaling them up will not reach reliable intelligence without symbols and reasoning. Others, such as Ed Zitron, see an investment bubble that will burst.

The doubt is as old as the field. In 1976 Joseph Weizenbaum warned against crediting machines with understanding they lack, and in 1980 John Searle’s Chinese room argued that running a program is not understanding. In 1973 the Lighthill report led Britain to cut its AI research.

Melanie Mitchell

Computer scientist at the Santa Fe Institute. Her essay “Why AI Is Harder Than We Think” (2021) set out the fallacies behind predictions of imminent human-level AI.

Noam Chomsky

Linguist. In 2023 he argued, with two colleagues, that ChatGPT finds patterns in data but cannot explain anything, unlike the human mind.

Promises

No new order: attention and money return to real problems. It echoes empiricism, back to Ada Lovelace’s objection (1843) that a machine can do only what we know how to order it to do.

If AI skepticism’s demands became law tomorrow, money and attention would pull back, and the bubble would deflate. The open question is who would absorb the losses.

Interests

Very little. Its best-known podcast, Mystery AI Hype Theater 3000, runs at the Distributed AI Research Institute, which the Ford and MacArthur foundations fund. Its other critics publish through books, newsletters and podcasts, with no major funders found.

Its demands could serve those who test claims and those who bet against them. Independent evaluators and academic critics gain, since demands for proof need someone to test the claims. Investors betting against the bubble gain if it bursts, and so, if the critics are right, do the established firms AI was meant to disrupt.

Rival research programmes gain, while start-ups that depend on AI funding would lose. Research itself can lose too: when the doubters last won, after the Lighthill report of 1973, British universities lost their AI funding. States, parties and patrons have little to take from it, since it asks for proof, not for action or money.

Its opponents could gain as well. A burst bubble would cut valuations, but it would leave the cash-rich giants standing as their rivals fail.

The Subprime AI Crisis

Ed Zitron

The writer argues that the generative AI industry loses money on its products and depends on a few cash-rich backers. When the money runs out, he predicts, the companies built on it will fail. His newsletter became the best-known voice of the bubble thesis.

Ed Zitron, “The Subprime AI Crisis”, Where's Your Ed At, September 2024

Too much spend, too little benefit?

Goldman Sachs

A Goldman Sachs research report asks whether the coming trillion dollars of AI investment will pay off. Its head of global equity research, Jim Covello, doubts it, since AI is expensive and has yet to solve the complex problems that would justify the cost.

Goldman Sachs, “Gen AI. Too Much Spend, Too Little Benefit?”, Top of Mind, issue 129, 25 June 2024

The False Promise of ChatGPT

Noam Chomsky, Ian Roberts and Jeffrey Watumull

In The New York Times, the linguist and two colleagues argue that ChatGPT works nothing like the human mind. It finds statistical patterns in vast data, they write, but cannot explain anything or tell what is impossible from what is merely unlikely.

Noam Chomsky, Ian Roberts and Jeffrey Watumull, “The False Promise of ChatGPT”, The New York Times, 8 March 2023

Mystery AI Hype Theater 3000

Emily M. Bender and Alex Hanna

The linguist and the sociologist start a livestream, later a podcast, in which they read and take apart AI hype. Hosted by the Distributed AI Research Institute, it has been downloaded more than 230,000 times.

Distributed AI Research Institute, “Mystery AI Hype Theater 3000”, dair-institute.org; Tech Policy Press, interview with Bender and Hanna, 2 July 2023

Deep Learning Is Hitting a Wall

Gary Marcus

The cognitive scientist argues that ever larger neural networks are reaching their limits on reliability and reasoning. Progress, he writes, needs a hybrid of neural networks with the symbols and logic of classical AI.

Gary Marcus, “Deep Learning Is Hitting a Wall”, Nautilus, 10 March 2022

Meaning, form and an octopus

Emily M. Bender and Alexander Koller

Two linguists argue that a system trained only on the form of language cannot learn its meaning. Their thought experiment has an octopus that taps an undersea cable and learns to mimic two people's messages, without knowing what any word refers to.

Emily M. Bender and Alexander Koller, “Climbing towards NLU. On Meaning, Form, and Understanding in the Age of Data”, Proceedings of ACL, 2020

Rebooting AI

Gary Marcus and Ernest Davis

Two NYU scientists argue that deep learning lacks common sense and an understanding of time, space and cause. Trustworthy AI, they write, will need knowledge built in, not only patterns learned from data.

Gary Marcus and Ernest Davis, Rebooting AI. Building Artificial Intelligence We Can Trust, Pantheon, 2019

Deep Learning. A Critical Appraisal

Gary Marcus

At the height of the deep-learning boom, Marcus lists ten of its limits, from the hunger for data to the failure to transfer what it learns. The paper set out the case that would follow every new model.

Gary Marcus, “Deep Learning. A Critical Appraisal”, arXiv 1801.00631, January 2018

The Chinese room

John Searle

The philosopher imagines himself in a room, following rules to answer questions in Chinese, a language he does not understand. To those outside he seems to understand. Running a program, he argues, is not the same as understanding.

John R. Searle, “Minds, Brains, and Programs”, Behavioral and Brain Sciences 3(3), 1980

Computer Power and Human Reason

Joseph Weizenbaum

The MIT computer scientist was alarmed that people confided in his simple chatbot ELIZA as if it understood them. In this book he warns against crediting machines with understanding, and argues that some decisions must stay with humans.

Joseph Weizenbaum, Computer Power and Human Reason. From Judgment to Calculation, W. H. Freeman, 1976

1973MilestoneDraft · to be checked

The Lighthill report

James Lighthill

Asked by Britain's Science Research Council to judge AI research, the Cambridge mathematician finds that it has failed to deliver on its promises. The report led to deep cuts in British AI funding. It is the precedent for what happens when the doubters win.

James Lighthill, “Artificial Intelligence. A General Survey”, Science Research Council, 1973

1972Claimed ancestor · citedDraft · to be checked

What Computers Can't Do

Hubert Dreyfus

The Berkeley philosopher argues that human intelligence rests on bodily know-how and context that cannot be written down as rules. The early hopes for AI, he concludes, rested on a mistaken picture of the mind.

Hubert L. Dreyfus, What Computers Can't Do. A Critique of Artificial Reason, Harper & Row, 1972
