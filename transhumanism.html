Transcendence · Introduction

Transhumanism

Transhumanism holds that the human condition is not fixed. Ageing, illness and the limits of the body and mind are problems that technology can solve, and should.

The hope is old: myths of immortality, Renaissance praise of human self-making, Enlightenment faith in endless improvement. The biologist Julian Huxley popularised the word in 1957. The movement took shape in the 1980s and 1990s, around the Extropians and, from 1998, the World Transhumanist Association, now Humanity+.

AI sits at its centre twice over. It could cure ageing and enhance the mind, and it may carry the mind itself, through brain implants or uploading. Many transhumanists also take the risks of AI seriously, and the movement shades into singularitarianism, rationalism and effective altruism.

Who holds it now

Humanity+

Non-profit that promotes transhumanism. Founded in 1998 as the World Transhumanist Association and renamed in 2008.

Max More

Philosopher. Gave transhumanism its modern definition in 1990 and co-founded the Extropy Institute.

Natasha Vita-More

Artist and writer. Chaired Humanity+, then served as its executive director until August 2024, when she became president emeritus.

Ray Kurzweil

Inventor and futurist. In 2024 he restated his forecasts of human-level AI by 2029 and a merger of human and machine intelligence by 2045.

Bryan Johnson

Entrepreneur. Founded Blueprint, a public attempt to slow his own ageing, and leads the “Don't Die” movement, which he links to superintelligent AI.

Neuralink

Brain–computer interface company, which implanted its device in a first human patient in 2024. Elon Musk has described its long-term aim as symbiosis with AI.

Who disputes it

Bioconservatives see a threat to human dignity and equality. In 2004 Foreign Policy asked one of them, Francis Fukuyama, to name one of the world's most dangerous ideas, and he chose transhumanism. “The first victim of transhumanism might be equality,” he wrote. The disability scholar Gregor Wolbring sees in it an ableism that treats every body as lacking and in need of endless improvement (2008). The acronym TESCREAL, coined by Émile P. Torres with Timnit Gebru, entered debate in 2023. Its T stands for transhumanism, first in a bundle of ideologies that the two trace to twentieth-century eugenics (2024).

2020s

2025Milestone

“Don't Die”

Bryan Johnson

Since 2021 the entrepreneur has followed Blueprint, a public regime to slow his own ageing. In January a Netflix documentary about him is released, and he sets out to found a Don't Die religion. He warns that superintelligent AI may soon move beyond human understanding and control. People must first choose not to die, he argues, and then make sure AI is aligned with human survival.

Jessica Hamzelou, “Bryan Johnson wants to start a new religion in which ‘the body is God’”, MIT Technology Review, 5 May 2025; Netflix Tudum, “Don't Die: The Man Who Wants to Live Forever”, 1 January 2025; “Bryan Johnson”, Wikipedia (Project Blueprint, announced October 2021)

VocabularyAlignment

2024Milestone

The Singularity Is Nearer

Ray Kurzweil

Nearly twenty years after The Singularity Is Near, Kurzweil restates his timeline. AI will reach and pass human-level intelligence by 2029, he writes, and by 2045 minds linked to the cloud will expand a millionfold. People careful with their health, he expects, will reach longevity escape velocity between 2029 and 2035. From then on, scientific progress would add at least a year of life for every year lived.

Ray Kurzweil, The Singularity Is Nearer: When We Merge with AI, Viking, 25 June 2024; Kurzweil's summary of the book for the Next Big Idea Club, 2024; Kirkus Reviews

2010s

2019Milestone

A stated aim for brain implants

Neuralink

At a launch event in San Francisco, Neuralink shows ultra-thin electrode threads and a robot that sews them into the brain. The first aim is medical. Elon Musk says the long-term goal is to “achieve a sort of symbiosis with artificial intelligence”, so humans are not left behind. In January 2024 he announces that a first human patient has received the implant.

Darrell Etherington, TechCrunch, 16 July 2019; Dezeen, 22 July 2019; Wyatte Grantham-Philips and Laura Ungar, Associated Press via PBS NewsHour, 30 January 2024

2000s

2005Milestone · its own genealogy

The movement writes its own history

Nick Bostrom

In “A History of Transhumanist Thought”, Bostrom traces the idea from the Epic of Gilgamesh through Pico della Mirandola and Condorcet to the Extropians. He names most of the older roots on this page as forerunners, from Gilgamesh to Ettinger.

Nick Bostrom, “A History of Transhumanist Thought”, Journal of Evolution and Technology 14(1), April 2005

1990s

1998Institutional root

The World Transhumanist Association

Nick Bostrom and David Pearce

Two philosophers found an association to promote transhumanism as a serious academic field. The Transhumanist Declaration, drafted that year by an international group, envisions broadening human potential by overcoming ageing, involuntary suffering and confinement to Earth. In 2008 the association renames itself Humanity+.

Nick Bostrom, “A History of Transhumanist Thought”, Journal of Evolution and Technology, 2005; Encyclopaedia Britannica, “World Transhumanist Association”; Humanity+, “The Transhumanist Declaration”

1990Modern definition

“Transhumanism: Toward a Futurist Philosophy”

Max More

Two years after launching the magazine Extropy with Tom Morrow, More gives the word its first modern definition. Transhumanism, he writes, is “a class of philosophies that seek to guide us towards a posthuman condition”. Humanity, in his view, is only a temporary stage on the path of evolution.

Max More, “Transhumanism: Toward a Futurist Philosophy”, Extropy no. 6, Summer 1990 (revised 1994 and 1996; republished on More's Substack, 2023); Nick Bostrom, “A History of Transhumanist Thought”, 2005

1960s

1962Claimed ancestor · cited

The Prospect of Immortality

Robert Ettinger

A physics teacher from Michigan proposes freezing the bodies of the dead until medicine can repair and revive them. The book, printed privately and then by Doubleday in 1964, launches cryonics. Ten years later Ettinger follows it with Man into Superman, on improving the human body itself.

Robert Ettinger, The Prospect of Immortality, privately printed, 1962; Doubleday, 1964; Man into Superman, 1972; obituary, Bangor Daily News, 25 July 2011

1950s

1957Claimed ancestor · popularised the word

“Transhumanism”

Julian Huxley

The biologist and first director-general of UNESCO uses the word for humanity “remaining man, but transcending himself” by realising new possibilities of its nature. The word had appeared before, in a 1940 paper by the Canadian philosopher W. D. Lighthall, but Huxley's essay made it known. Huxley was also a prominent supporter of eugenics, and president of the Eugenics Society from 1959 to 1962, a link the movement's critics stress.

Julian Huxley, “Transhumanism”, in New Bottles for New Wine, Chatto & Windus, 1957; “Transhumanism” and “Julian Huxley”, Wikipedia (Lighthall, Eugenics Society)

1920s

1929Claimed ancestor · cited

The World, the Flesh and the Devil

J. D. Bernal

A young Cambridge crystallographer imagines people replacing their bodies with machinery, the brain living on inside a short cylinder. He pictures minds linked together and hollow globes, ten miles across, settled in space. Bostrom's history counts it among the forerunners of transhumanism.

J. D. Bernal, The World, the Flesh and the Devil, Kegan Paul, Trench, Trubner, 1929 (To-day and To-morrow series); The Encyclopedia of Science Fiction, “Bernal, J D”

1923Claimed ancestor · cited

Daedalus, or Science and the Future

J. B. S. Haldane

In a paper read to the Heretics Society in Cambridge, published the next year, Haldane predicts children grown outside the womb. The centre of scientific interest, he argues, lies in biology, which may one day alter our own species. His friend Aldous Huxley drew on these ideas in Brave New World (1932).

J. B. S. Haldane, Daedalus, or Science and the Future, a paper read to the Heretics, Cambridge, on 4 February 1923, Kegan Paul, Trench, Trubner, 1924; Aubrey Pinteric, Embryo Project Encyclopedia, 2025

1900s

1906Attributed by historians

The Philosophy of the Common Task

Nikolai Fedorov

Two volumes of the Moscow librarian's writings appear after his death, in 1906 and 1913, edited by his disciples. Humanity's common task, he argues, is to master nature, defeat death and bring every ancestor back to life. Seen as the founder of Russian cosmism, he inspired the rocket pioneer Konstantin Tsiolkovsky and is read today as a forerunner of transhumanism.

N. F. Fedorov, Filosofiia obshchego dela, ed. V. A. Kozhevnikov and N. P. Peterson, vol. 1, 1906; vol. 2, 1913; S. G. Semenova, “Fëdorov, Nikolai Fëdorovich”, Encyclopedia of Philosophy, 2nd edn, 2005

Before 1800

1795Claimed ancestor · cited

Sketch for a Historical Picture of the Progress of the Human Mind

Condorcet

Written in hiding during the Terror and published after his death, the essay foresees the indefinite improvement of humanity. People will not become immortal, Condorcet concedes, but the span of human life may have no fixed limit. Bostrom quotes this passage as an early hope of extending life through medicine.

Condorcet, Esquisse d'un tableau historique des progrès de l'esprit humain, 1795; Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Marie-Jean-Antoine-Nicolas de Caritat, marquis de Condorcet”; Nick Bostrom, “A History of Transhumanist Thought”, 2005

1486Claimed ancestor · cited

Oration on the Dignity of Man

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola

Written in 1486 to introduce 900 theses for a public debate, the speech was printed only in 1496, after Pico's death. In it God tells Adam that humans alone have no fixed nature. They can make themselves whatever they choose to be.

Pico della Mirandola, Oratio de hominis dignitate, written 1486, first printed in his Commentationes, ed. Gianfrancesco Pico, 1496; Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, “Giovanni Pico della Mirandola”

c. 1800 BCEClaimed ancestor · cited

The Epic of Gilgamesh

Anonymous, Mesopotamia

The first epic of the king of Uruk is written in Akkadian, drawing on Sumerian poems from about 2100 BCE. After the death of his friend Enkidu, Gilgamesh searches for the secret of eternal life. In the later standard version he finds a plant that restores youth, and loses it to a snake.

Old Babylonian epic, 18th century BCE; Sumerian poems from the Third Dynasty of Ur, c. 2100 BCE; standard Babylonian version by Sîn-lēqi-unninni, c. 1300–1000 BCE. Ira Spar, “Gilgamesh”, Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History, Metropolitan Museum of Art, 2009; “Epic of Gilgamesh”, Wikipedia
