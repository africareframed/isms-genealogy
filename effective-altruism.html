Risk · Introduction

Effective altruism

Includes longtermism

Effective altruism holds that we should use evidence and reason to do the most good we can. Longtermists also count the generations to come, and many now see risk from advanced AI as the most pressing.

Its roots are utilitarian. In 1972 Peter Singer argued that a starving child far away has the same claim on us as a drowning child nearby. GiveWell (2007) and Giving What We Can (2009) turned the question into practice, and in 2011 the movement named itself effective altruism.

Longtermism followed: Nick Bostrom argued that extinction would forfeit a vast future, and in 2017 William MacAskill named the view. AI moved to the centre, funded since 2015 by Open Philanthropy and, from 2025, the main focus of 80,000 Hours. The collapse of FTX in 2022, run by one of the movement’s major donors, badly damaged its reputation.

Who holds it now

William MacAskill

Philosopher and co-founder of Giving What We Can, 80,000 Hours and the Centre for Effective Altruism. At Forethought he studies how to navigate the transition to superintelligent AI.

Toby Ord

Philosopher, co-founder of Giving What We Can and author of The Precipice. A senior researcher at Oxford's AI Governance Initiative.

Coefficient Giving

Called Open Philanthropy until November 2025. By 2025 it had helped Good Ventures, the foundation of Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna, give over $4 billion. It has funded AI safety since 2015.

80,000 Hours

Careers organisation founded in 2011. Since April 2025 it focuses on helping people make the transition to advanced AI go well.

Centre for Effective Altruism

Runs the EA Global conferences and the EA Forum. Its chief executive, Zachary Robinson, stresses that the movement is not the same as AI safety.

Giving What We Can

Its members pledge at least 10% of their income to effective charities. More than 11,000 had taken its 10% Pledge by November 2026.

Who disputes it

Amia Srinivasan argued that the movement “leaves everything just as it is”, ignoring power and the deep sources of global misery (2015). Jim Holt warned that refusing to discount the future could make us “moral slaves to that future” (2021). Émile P. Torres called longtermism possibly “the most dangerous secular belief system” (2021); with Timnit Gebru, Torres placed it in the TESCREAL bundle (2024). David Sacks, then Trump's AI adviser, called EA-linked groups a “Doomer Industrial Complex” pushing for tougher AI rules (2025).

2020s

2026Milestone

“Americanism, not effective altruism”

The US Department of War and the Centre for Effective Altruism

In mid-September a US Department of War account posted the slogan on X, and a senior Pentagon official called EA's ideology dangerous. Anthropic, many of whose co-founders have EA ties, became a particular target, though it does not publicly align with the movement. In November Zachary Robinson, head of the Centre for Effective Altruism, said EA is not the same as AI safety. Global health, he noted, has long received much more of its money than AI.

Beatrice Nolan and Emily Forlini, “How ‘Effective Altruism’ went from fringe philosophy to Trump's AI villain”, Fortune, 23 September 2026; Peter Singer, “Trump's Misguided War Against Effective Altruism”, Project Syndicate, 13 October 2026; Thomas Urbain, “Under fire over AI, effective altruism wants to tell its own story”, AFP, 9 November 2026

2025Milestone

80,000 Hours turns to AGI

80,000 Hours

The careers organisation founded by William MacAskill and Benjamin Todd narrows its focus to making the transition to advanced AI go well. It judges that AGI could plausibly arrive within the next decade. Reducing AI risk, it argues, is probably the best way to do the most good over the long term. New content on other topics now faces a much higher bar.

Niel Bowerman and the 80,000 Hours team, “We're shifting our strategic approach to focus more on AGI”, 80,000 Hours, 4 April 2025; 80,000 Hours, “About us”, 80000hours.org

2023Milestone

The OpenAI board crisis

Helen Toner, Tasha McCauley and the OpenAI board

OpenAI's non-profit board removed its chief executive, Sam Altman, saying he had not been consistently candid with it. Two directors had ties to effective altruism: Helen Toner had worked at Open Philanthropy, and Tasha McCauley sat on the board of Effective Ventures. Critics blamed the movement, though in September OpenAI had said none of its board members were effective altruists. After nearly all staff threatened to leave, Altman returned within days, and both women left the board.

Matt O'Brien, “OpenAI's ex-chairman accuses board of going rogue in firing Altman”, Associated Press via Fortune, 18 November 2023; Matt O'Brien, “OpenAI brings back Sam Altman as CEO just days after his firing unleashed chaos”, Associated Press, 22 November 2023; Semafor, “The AI industry turns against its favorite philosophy”, 21 November 2023; Center for Security and Emerging Technology, “Helen Toner”, staff page

2022Milestone

The fall of FTX

Sam Bankman-Fried and the FTX Future Fund

In 2012 MacAskill had pitched earning to give to Sam Bankman-Fried, then a student, who became a leading funder of the movement's causes. In November 2022 his crypto exchange, FTX, collapsed. The team of its Future Fund, which funded work on AI and humanity's long-term prospects, resigned, MacAskill among them. Bankman-Fried was convicted of fraud in 2023 and sentenced to 25 years, and the episode badly damaged the movement's reputation.

Hillary Hoffower, “Sam Bankman-Fried was an effective altruism evangelist, pledging to give away most of his $26 billion fortune…”, Fortune, 15 November 2022; Justin Weinberg, “Cryptocurrency Chaos Affects Academic Grants (updated)”, Daily Nous, 11 November 2022; Ken Sweet and Larry Neumeister, “FTX founder Sam Bankman-Fried sentenced to 25 years in prison”, Associated Press, 28 March 2024; Thomas Urbain, “Under fire over AI, effective altruism wants to tell its own story”, AFP, 9 November 2026

2022Milestone

What We Owe the Future

William MacAskill

MacAskill rests longtermism on three claims: future people count, there could be very many of them, and we can make their lives better. Among the risks he weighs are man-made pathogens and advanced AI. AI, he warns, could also lock in one set of values for a very long time. TIME ran a cover story on the book and the movement.

William MacAskill, What We Owe the Future, Basic Books, 16 August 2022; Krister Bykvist, review, Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews, 7 July 2023; Naina Bajekal, “Want to Do More Good? This Movement Might Have the Answer”, TIME, 10 August 2022

2020Milestone

The Precipice

Toby Ord

An Oxford philosopher argues that since the first atomic bomb, in 1945, humanity has lived in an age of heightened existential risk. He puts the chance of an existential catastrophe this century at about one in six. The largest single risk, about one in ten, is AI whose goals do not match human flourishing. Reducing such risk, he argues, should be a major global priority.

Toby Ord, The Precipice: Existential Risk and the Future of Humanity, Bloomsbury, London, and Hachette, New York, 2020; Theron Pummer, review, Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews, 2 August 2020

2010s

2017Claimed ancestor · coined the word

“Longtermism”

William MacAskill, with Toby Ord and Hilary Greaves

Ord's book in progress lent urgency to a search for a name. In October 2017 MacAskill proposed “longtermism”, for the view that our actions matter most through their effects on the very long-run future. With Hilary Greaves he later made the case for strong longtermism, putting the expected number of future beings at a trillion trillion or more. Among the ways to shape that future, they discuss guiding the development of superintelligence.

William MacAskill, “‘Longtermism’”, Effective Altruism Forum, 25 July 2019; Hilary Greaves and William MacAskill, “The Case for Strong Longtermism”, GPI Working Paper, Global Priorities Institute, June 2021

2016Milestone

Open Philanthropy makes AI risk a priority

Holden Karnofsky, Open Philanthropy

GiveWell's offshoot, which advises the foundation of Dustin Moskovitz and Cari Tuna, makes potential risks from advanced AI “a major priority”. Karnofsky gives at least a one-in-ten chance of transformative AI within twenty years, and finds the cause important, neglected and tractable. In March 2017 it granted OpenAI $30 million over three years, and Karnofsky joined OpenAI's board.

Holden Karnofsky, “Potential Risks from Advanced Artificial Intelligence: The Philanthropic Opportunity”, Open Philanthropy, 6 May 2016; Open Philanthropy, “OpenAI — General Support”, March 2017; GiveWell, “Our story”, givewell.org

2011Claimed ancestor · coined the word

“Effective altruism”

William MacAskill and the Oxford founders

Giving What We Can was growing, and 80,000 Hours, a careers project, had soft-launched in February. Planning an umbrella charity, the founders needed a name for people using their lives to do good effectively. MacAskill pushed for “effective altruism”, and on 5 December a shortlist vote chose the Centre for Effective Altruism by a clear margin. Peter Singer's 2013 TED talk later spread the term.

William MacAskill, “The history of the term ‘effective altruism’”, Effective Altruism Forum, 11 March 2014

2000s

2009Institutional root

Giving What We Can

Toby Ord, Bernadette Young and William MacAskill

Inspired by ethicists such as Peter Singer, the Oxford philosopher Toby Ord decides to give a large share of his income to effective charities. With his wife, Bernadette Young, and William MacAskill, he launches a society whose members pledge at least 10% of their income. By November 2026 more than 11,000 people had taken its 10% Pledge.

Giving What We Can, “About us” and “Our history”, givingwhatwecan.org, seen 20 November 2026

2007Institutional root

GiveWell

Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld

Eight friends working in finance try to find out where their donations would do most good, but foundations will not share what they know. Two of them, Holden Karnofsky and Elie Hassenfeld, leave their jobs and launch GiveWell full time. It recommends a few top charities, chosen for strong evidence and high cost-effectiveness, and publishes its research. It also incubated Open Philanthropy, which became separate in 2017.

GiveWell, “Our story”, givewell.org, updated February 2018; GiveWell, “Quick Start Guide”, givewell.org

2003Claimed ancestor · cited

“Astronomical Waste”

Nick Bostrom

On a cautious count, Bostrom estimates, each second's delay in settling our local supercluster loses over ten trillion potential human lives. Yet a single percentage point less existential risk would be worth more than ten million years of delay. For utilitarians, he concludes, the rule becomes “Minimize existential risk!” Greaves and MacAskill cite the paper in their case for strong longtermism.

Nick Bostrom, “Astronomical Waste: The Opportunity Cost of Delayed Technological Development”, Utilitas 15(3), 2003, pp. 308–314; Hilary Greaves and William MacAskill, “The Case for Strong Longtermism”, GPI Working Paper, June 2021

2002Modern definition

“Existential Risks”

Nick Bostrom

The philosopher defines an existential risk as one that would “annihilate Earth-originating intelligent life or permanently and drastically curtail its potential”. Among such risks he lists a badly programmed superintelligence, given goals that lead it to destroy humankind. Reducing them, he argues, should be a dominant consideration, at least as a rule of thumb. A 2020 EA Forum guide sets it beside the newer definition in Ord's The Precipice.

Nick Bostrom, “Existential Risks: Analyzing Human Extinction Scenarios and Related Hazards”, Journal of Evolution and Technology 9(1), 2002; Michael Aird, “Clarifying existential risks and existential catastrophes”, Effective Altruism Forum, 24 April 2020

1980s

1984Claimed ancestor · cited

Reasons and Persons

Derek Parfit

Parfit compares three outcomes: peace, a nuclear war that kills 99% of the world's people, and a war that kills everyone. The gap between the last two is far greater, he argues, because extinction would also end all the generations to come. Destroying mankind, he writes, would be “much worse than most people think”. The passage has led many effective altruists to work on extinction risk.

Derek Parfit, Reasons and Persons, Clarendon Press, Oxford, 1984, pp. 453–454; Effective Altruism Forum, “Derek Parfit” and “Ethics of existential risk”, wiki entries

1970s

1972Claimed ancestor · cited

“Famine, Affluence, and Morality”

Peter Singer

Writing in November 1971, as people die in East Bengal for lack of food, Singer imagines a child drowning in a shallow pond. He ought to wade in, he says, even at the cost of muddy clothes. If we can prevent something bad without sacrificing “anything of comparable moral importance”, he argues, we ought to, however far away the suffering. Giving What We Can credits the essay with inspiring the modern effective giving movement.

Peter Singer, “Famine, Affluence, and Morality”, Philosophy & Public Affairs 1(3), 1972, pp. 229–243; full text and introduction on the Giving What We Can website

1870s

1874Claimed ancestor · cited

The Methods of Ethics

Henry Sidgwick

A Cambridge lecturer sets out utilitarianism, the theory Jeremy Bentham first stated clearly. The time at which a person lives, he argues, cannot change the value of their happiness. So the interests of posterity “must concern a Utilitarian as much as those of his contemporaries”. Greaves and MacAskill cite him among those who endorse a zero rate of pure time preference.

Henry Sidgwick, The Methods of Ethics, Macmillan, London, 1874, Book IV, ch. 1, § 2, p. 385; Hilary Greaves and William MacAskill, “The Case for Strong Longtermism”, GPI Working Paper 5-2021, June 2021, note 21
